The president is one major component of the **executive branch**. By design, they are not to be a king, but the vesting clause does allow them declare some powers. Stewardship presidents took advantage of this, unlike the early constructionist presidents.

They are selected by the electoral college. General elections weren't considered because the infrastructure at the time of the U.S.' founding couldn't support same-day elections. Likewise, having Congress select would go against the essence of democracy.

The president prematurely goes out of power through death, impeachment, resignation, or simply deemed unfit by the vice president and majority of the Cabinet. The vice president takes over and gets the title 'accidental president.' The 25th Amendment formalized the procedure. The spirit of the 25th Amendment describes temporary transfer of presidential powers when the president would be inactive for a short while.



# {{< heading "Powers" >}}
They are head of government. They nominate officials and have presidential veto. They are Commander in Chief, meaning they are in charge of the military and have authority over troops. Convening Congress will bring Congress back into session. And Executive Orders allow the president to make something lawesque without going through Congress, but the courts can challenge it. They also negotiate treaties and propose the annual budget—a list of what they want to spend money on.

{{< subtext >}}
    Senatorial courtesy is when the president lets relevant senators know who they are considering for the position.

    Recess appointments don't require the Senate's approval, and they can be made when the Senate goes on recess until the end of the house representative cycle. 

    The president is the Commander in Chief because the Framers wanted an elected civilian at the top of military.

    Executive agreements have become the norm over treaties as they don't require the Senate's approval. They are not a direct replacement, however.

    Presidents attach Signing Statements to a law detailing how they will implement it. 
{{< /subtext >}}

On the Commander in Chief role in particular, the War Powers Act seeks to restrict the president. It requires that they notify Congress within 48 hours of committing troops to a combat zone. Then these troops can only be there for 60 days.

The president is also the Chief of State, *the* representative of the nation. They can pardon someone to erase their federal conviction and punishments. Or they grant clemency to end a prison sentence early while keeping the conviction. As the public opinion leader, media will write about the president's unique priorities, and people will talk about it. With all eyes on them, the president may employ the Going Public idea: encouraging the people to pressure Congress to do some thing.

{{< subtext >}}
    Pardoning is the only unchecked power of the president.
{{< /subtext >}}

Unfortunately, the president always straddles public approval. In fact, public approval always decays, which is known as the bank account presidency. Rally around the Flag can reverse this; in a crisis, Americans will get really patriotic and really support the president. On the other end, a president may "claim executive privilege" to stop the release of information that they believe may threaten their job or even the U.S. This tends to get misused, and the courts strike it down. 



# {{< heading "Institutional Presidency" >}}
The U.S. follows a hierarchy model. At the top is the Chief of Staff, who coordinates information around to the president and around the White House.

The West Wing of the White House is organized into the Executive Office of the President (EOP). They support the president in their duties. The White House Office (WHO) is one component of the EOP, and it consists of staffers that interact with the president daily and most frequently. The Office of Management and Budget (OMB) processes all reports and oversight testimonies to perform central clearance—ensure it is consistent with the president's beliefs (regardless of one's own).

The Cabinet is comprised of departments focused on a particular issue. Cabinet secretaries, the ones in charge of their respective department, were originally advisors to the president, but are now largely managers and gives briefs to the president. They are chosen by the president, typically because they share the same beliefs.

{{< subtext >}}
    The Cabinet is not mandated by the Constitution, but since the departments are created by Congress, the president cannot do away with it.

    Capture theory says that the president grows distrustful of their cabinet because the secretaries slowly change their beliefs as they understand the department.
{{< /subtext >}}
