Although tariffs are paid by the importer, this price is often passed onto consumers. However, they do bring government revenue, protect domestic business, protect domestic jobs, encourage foreign companies to build domestic factories, or be used as bargaining leverage (threaten cutting access to domestic consumers for the foreign nation).

{{< subtext >}}
    Domestic jobs are protected by helping local companies stay profitable, reducing the pressure to cut wages or move production overseas.
{{< /subtext >}}

Not only do tariffs raise the price of foreign goods, but domestic producers can feel too comfortable and raise their own prices. Choices are also reduced because foreign producers export less and retailers find it too expensive. There are also job losses in downstream industries because they cannot fully pass the price of the tariff of their inputs, thus cutting back on employment. Retaliatory tariffs can hurt domestic exporters.  Retaliatory tariffs can hurt domestic exporters. 



# {{< heading "Trump" >}}
Tariffs had been low after WWII in support of free trade. Trump raised this to relatively astronomical levels. He's using tariffs to rebalance global trade and as a revenue tool. He also claimed that he could impose tariffs in an immediate answer with no review from Congress. 

The Supreme Court ruled against Trump, which is significant because this Supreme Court has been quite friendly towards Trump. The *International Economic Emergency Powers Act* (IEEPA) allowed presidents to regulate trade after declaring a national emergency involving foreign threats. Although IEEPA does not explicitly mention tariffs, Trump argued that it still granted him the power to levy tariffs. The ruling was rooted on the fact that Congress possesses the constitutional power to enact tariffs and that Congress can lend this power to the president if it does so clearly. 

{{< subtext >}}
    75% of tariff revenue came from these now-illegal tariffs. 
{{< /subtext >}}

Trump enacted a new sweeping tariff on nearly all imports using the Trade Act of 1974. This act is more limited than IEEPA, meaning it is only in effect for only 150 days unless extended by Congress, which is unlikely. Additionally, the tariffs can only be applied non-discriminatory, meaning it is not possible to target certain countries. Section 232 or Section 301 are other options to apply tariffs. However, in general, it will be more difficult and slower than IEEPA. 

There looks like there will be refunds for importers for the tariffs they paid that are now struck down. Trading partners made significant concessions for lower tariff rates under IEEPA, but now they are hit with a higher tariff nonetheless. Additionally, the bargaining power of tariffs disappear. There is also uncertainty domestically and internationally. This is a struggle for business and consumers as they plan for future spending.
