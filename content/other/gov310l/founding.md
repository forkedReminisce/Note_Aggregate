The Pilgrams established a theocracy, a religious government. They also operated under compacts, which legally binds the consenting persons to enforceable rules. 

Due to its British roots, the 13 colonies' governments were inspired by Great Britain's government. This is why, today, Congress is bicameral and the law-making procedure is similar to the U.K.'s.

When the Declaration of Independence was written, it brought up ideas that would later be in the Constitution. Equality, natural rights, and legitimacy. Natural rights (i.e., life, liberty, and the pursuit of happiness) had to be protected by the government. There was also social contract, which means that people give up some autonomy and freedom to be governed and treated well.

After the Revolution, the U.S. started with the Articles of Confederation. A confederation is a system of government where the national government is overpowered by the states. As one can imagine, the nation was ineffective and states became very individualist.   

The wealthy paid for the Revolution, and they wanted to be paid back. The states, in turn, raised property taxes. The farmers, who had been fighting the war and not tending their now-disheveled fields, had to foreclose on their farms. Daniel Shays starts a riot in Massachusetts. 

**Shays's Rebellion** and the lack of rescue scares the states and the wealthy so much that 40 delegates immediately participate in the Constitutional Convention to replace the Articles. The Founders were elites well-versed in classic philosophy. They agreed on a stronger national government.

# {{< heading "Writing The Constitution" >}}
State representation because a topic of debate. The Virginia Plan sought to proportion a state's influence in the national government by its population. On the other end was the New Jersey Plan, which gave every state a single vote. The Great Compromise (Connecticut Compromise) created a bicameral legislature (Congress) with a chamber implementing one or the other system.

Presidential elections for the country will happen on the same day across the nation. Election logistics are handled by the state. The number of votes each state gets is the sum of its House members and Senators. Whoever gets the majority of total electors wins presidency. If no one got the majority, the House decided. 

Other important parts of the Constitution were:
- Supremacy clause: the national government has the final say over the state government
- Elastic clause (necessary and proper clause): the government must be able to adapt with an everchanging society
- Full Faith and Credit Clause: legal documents of one state must be recognized by every other state

{{< subtext >}}
    Enumerated powers are explicitly given by the Constitution. Implied powers can be argued for.
{{< /subtext >}}

The government the Framers created was republic and federalist. It exhibited separation of powers between Congress, the presidency, and the courts. There was also checks and balances that allowed one of these entities to stop an action by another.

Ratification of the Constitution was debated by the federalists and anti-federalists. The anti-federalists desperately feared an overreaching government like Great Britain's. They would only change their minds with a Bill of Rights. This was an easy concession for the federalists. The Constitution would get ratified, and most Americans accepted this new government quickly. 

{{< subtext >}}
    The Bill of Rights was redundant as every state had its own version. However, anti-federalists wanted the protections to be guaranteed across the nation in the case a state got rid of its bill of rights.
{{< /subtext >}}



# {{< heading "Changing The Constitution" >}}
*Formal amendment* to the Constitution can either be done via national convention or a procedure. The former is never done, so the focus will be on the procedure.
1. A constitutional amendment proposal goes to both houses of Congress
2. The proposal gets a 2/3s vote (extraordinary majority) from both houses
3. 3/4s of all states' legislatures must vote yes.

{{< subtext >}}
    Special ratifying conventions in every state is the final method to amending the Constitution. 3/4s of all states must vote yes.

    The high thresholds ensure deliberative will—that the amendment was truly necessary and wasn't particularly helped by the heat of the moment.
{{< /subtext >}}

*Judicial interpretation* is another way to change the constitution. Not in the traditional sense since it's the Supreme Court deciding how to interpret parts of the Constitution.
