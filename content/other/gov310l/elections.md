# {{< heading "History" >}}
At the founding, only white landowning males could vote. Landowning because those people were attached to the region. A lot of white men did not own land and could, therefore, not vote.

Rich but not landowning males wanted to vote. So they went west and got land. States that would then establish here would remove the landowning restriction on eligibility. This leads to the Jacksonian era, where all the states offered universal white male suffrage. This made the voter base less wealthy, too.

The 15th Amendment removed the race restriction. Women were given the right to vote in the 19th Amendment. The 23rd Amendment gave the District of Columbia (D.C.) electoral college votes. The 26th Amendment brought the age requirement down from 21 to 18. This was because they were basically adults and also were eligible for the draft. 



# {{< heading "Participation" >}}
People with more education are more likely to vote. Likewise, older people are likely to vote. This is because younger people tend to be busier and growing more concerned about taxes. At around 60, this tends to turn around.

{{< subtext >}}
    Older people vote less because they can't find transportation to the polls or license renewal.
{{< /subtext >}}

Southern states are slightly less likely to vote. This is because they tend to have non-partisan ballots, removing the party cue of each candidate. Minorities are less likely to vote. This is probably because they are turned off by the fact that there is not somebody who looks like them on the ballot. 

People with strong community ties (e.g., married) are more likely to vote because policies concern them more. People who are strongly partisan are moe likely to vote. Wealth is positively correlated with the chance to vote. More women tend to vote because they live longer. 

Participation is not only voting. Spectator activities is promoting a way to vote (e.g., a pin). Trying to personally convince people to vote a certain way. Contacting a public official. Candidate donation. Volunteering in a political campaign. After that is climbing the ranks and taking office.

Get out the Vote (GOTV) or the ground game is encouraging people to vote. Direct mobilization is reaching out to people to a personal level. Indirect mobilization is getting people to encourage their peers.


## {{< heading "Low Turnout" >}}
A reason why Americans don't vote as much as those in other countries is that they have to manually register to vote and it must match the area they live in. Additionally, since there's so many elections, there's voter fatigue. 

People are too busy. They also may just not like any candidate.

Many polls are only open during working hours typically on Tuesdays. Some people cannot sacrifice pay. Placement can also be strategically done to one candidate's benefit. Some areas implement closed polling, which assigns voters to a particular polling location where they must vote.  

{{< subtext >}}
    Elections are held on Tuesdays because the weekend is used by different religions.
{{< /subtext >}}


## {{< heading "Cues" >}}
The biggest clue to how someone will vote is the party they belong to. The better-looking candidate tends to win. Incumbency heavily benefits a candidate because a new candidate brings uncertainty. People also tend to vote for candidates that have personally helped them (e.g., casework). The candidate's characteristics (e.g., age) also influence the vote.

Prospective Voting is voting for someone based on their promises. The problem is that it's really easy to say stuff but hard to execute because of the other branches. Retrospective Voting is when a candidate is judged by their past performance, even if they hadn't held that particular position or were just simply associated with another administration. The problem is that officials will devise policies that are popular short-term but bad long-term (e.g., lowering taxes).

Some people are single issue voters. This means they vote for the candidate that align with their views on that issue, regardless of how that candidate thinks of other issues. 

Candidates can sway the vote by trying to connect with the public. However, this can backfire if executed poorly. Doing well in debates can also help a run, especially for underdogs. Negative Campaigning is attacking the opponent. These insults tend to stick in people's heads, and there's also a conditioning effect. This exploits the fact that most people don't care and simple and repetitive messages works.



# {{< heading "Electoral College" >}}
America uses indirect elections. This is because at the founding, infrastructure was primitive, and in order to hold a national election across states on the same day, the states would have to hold their elections. Direct elections would be ideal, but it requires a lot of work. The national government needs to fund equalizing voting equipment across every county and local officials need to be better supported (indirect elections allow them to prematurely stop counting if the other side winning is projected to be impossible).

The Electoral College encourages campaigning in only big states because they have the biggest portion of electoral votes. Particularly, swing states. States whose leaning are solidly known are neglected. Remember, winning the popular vote does not matter.

Almost all states operate on a winner take all system for the Electoral College. Nebraska gives each congressional district a separate vote. The two Senator votes are by popular vote across the state. 

In Texas, each political party select a number of Electors that is equal to the number of electoral votes of the state. These Electors are typically very loyal party members that will definitely vote for the party's candidate. After all states make final decisions for who will get what electoral votes. Back to Texas, the slate of Electors of the party that won meet at the state capital and signs forms of certification. 

Because a candidate must get a majority of all the electoral votes, if this isn't hit, Congress holds an internal vote. The House decides the president, and each state, not each member, gets only one vote. The Senate decides teh vice president, and each senator gets one vote. 



# {{< heading "Financing Campaigns" >}}
It wasn't until the last 50 years that regulations were put into place. The Federal Campaign Act (FECA) put a cap on how much one could donate. However, PACs, Political Action Committees, have a higher cap. The Federal Election Commission made it easier to form PACs. 

{{< subtext >}}
    SuperPACs can raise and spend unlimited amounts of money for support or opposing political candidates. They can directly attack a political candidate. They cannot directly coordinate with a candidate or their party, but the line is very fuzzy.
{{< /subtext >}}

Parties often coordinate spending. This covers when candidates will take a particular stance in order to gain funding. Alternatively, a candidate may engage in independent spending. Candidates here will spend their own money. 

There are two types of donations. Hard money is used directly for the campaign. However, soft money is used for registration, education, and mobilization of the people. This is unregulated and now outlawed.

Supreme Court case Citizens United (2010) removed restrictions on donations by any organization. This potentially opened the door for individuals to donate under the guise of a corporation. Additionally, there emerged another way of donating. The oldest way is donating directly to the candidate, which is under restrictions and must be publicly disclosed. The new way is sending money to another entity officially unaffiliated with the campaign and party, which makes the money not under the same level of scrutiny. 

Senatorial candidates tend to spend more money on TV ads. This is because they cover the entire state. House of Representatives candidates use more targeted advertising because they will represent a congressional district. 

Money can be self-reinforcing. As in, people want to make smart choices with their money. Therefore, people will donate to candidates they think will have a good shot at winning.


## {{< heading "Tax Code Loopholes" >}}
527s are tax-exempt organizations are a loophole that allows them to use soft money. They influence elections through voter mobilization or issue ads—promote one side of an issue and imply support to the candidate that holds that belief. 

They must report finances to the IRS, unless they've already done it at the state or local level. This allows the people running the 527 to stay anonymous. However, since they cannot coordinate with the candidate, they tend to have been already quite close to them.

501 (c) are non-profit, tax exempt groups. 501 (c) (3) cannot engage in political activity but can engage in some voter registration. 501 (c) (4) are social welfare organizations that can engage in political activity as long as it is not their main purpose. They can lobby for legislation, participate in campaigns, and donors do not need to be publicly disclosed. Even though it's not supposed to be its main purpose, for some, political activity is for the election cycle. When they get investigated for it, they say they will wind down after the election cycle, then bankrupt after the election cycle.
