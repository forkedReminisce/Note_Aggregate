Greenland offers a strategic position between North America and Europe, wedging itself in between, and it is also close to Russia, which is valuable for air defense. It is a self-governing territory of Denmark. Denmark is responsible for foreign policy and defense, and it also provides a grant for public services. There is significant U.S. military presence on Greenland.

Greenland has played a significant role in U.S. defense since WWII. So important, in fact, that the U.S. had tried to buy it all those years ago. When wind hit that the USSR was developing nuclear bombs, the 1951 Defense of Greenland Agreement was signed between the U.S. and Denmark to allow greater U.S. military presence on the territory. 

Greenland also has economic value. It sits in the center of Arctic shipping lanes, which are significantly shorter to Asia, and climate changing is creating even more lanes around the country for longer periods of time year-round. Additionally, the alternate route, the Suez Canal, requires going through the volatile Middle East. 

The country also has some of the world's largest deposits of rare earth minerals used in high tech industries. So much so that Greenland could challenge China's global dominance in rare earth minerals. However, the climate, geography, and strict environmental regulations makes mining difficult. Additionally, the infrastructure and people are not there. Though, it still has the potential to be very profitable. 

The way President Trump starts and goes about crises has become predictable. The first stage, the prelude, Trump will announce a provocative position enough to get international attention. Escalation is when Trump makes threats, increasing the perceived urgency, prompting responses from others, and implies that the administration is willing to go further. Resolution is where the U.S. steps back and a negotiated settlement comes, often unexpectedly, and the U.S. claims it as a success. What follows is fallout, where trust in the U.S. erodes and everyone has strategic doubt. 

In regards to Greenland, the prelude happened at the end of Trump's first term. In his second term, the U.S. got much more aggressive on this stance. Escalation was marked with Trump refusing to rule out the use of military force for Greenland. The U.S.' invasion of Venezuela (and messaging suggesting Greenland was next) led European leaders to take threats against Greenland seriously. European countries sent NATO military personnel to Greenland, and Trump threatened to place a tariff on them. 

Meetings between Denmark, Greenland, and the U.S. ended with Greenland wishing to stay with Denmark, making it clear to the U.S. no one was going to budge. Additionally, the Republican party was starting to falter and the stock market plummeted. Trump then walked back on the use of military force, and tariffs after discussing a potential agreement on Greenland with NATO. 

The Greenland crisis has irreversibly changed the international order. The world has entered a new period of great power conflict, where the great powers will use their capabilities to pursue their interests without any regard to international rules. Although American hegemony is over, it has brought some genuinely beneficial things. It's now time for middle powers to no longer assume great powers will stick to the rules and to start taking responsibility for their security and prosperity. Middle powers must start building strength, developing resilient economies to reduce the leverage of great powers, diversify partnerships, and to band together and act collectively.
