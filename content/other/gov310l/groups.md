Interest groups are private groups that want to influence a particular type of public policy (e.g., gun policies). They are lobbies, and they often have lobbyists who primary goal is to lobby—try to influence public policy by contacting public officials.

{{< subtext >}}
    Other names for interest groups are special interests, lobby groups, public interest groups, and pressure groups.
{{< /subtext >}}



# {{< heading "Types" >}}
A large portion of interest groups are businesses. They do this to maximize revenue. They often have professional (employed) lobbyists.

{{< subtext >}}
    Temporary lobbyists exist and they're hired much like how lawyers at law firms are.
{{< /subtext >}}

Unions and Professional Organizations (e.g., American Bar Association) will often interact with legislatures. Ideological Groups focus on a perspective, while Single Issue Groups have their issue. Public Interest Groups are tax exempt charities, and they prioritize public good. 

Centralized groups have a headquarters and field offices all over the nation. Confederations give more freedom to the local level, which may result in some discrepancies with the national side.

Peak associations membership is comprised of businesses, not individuals. Mass associations have due-paying members. This lends to checkbook membership, where an interest group will set their dues low in order to boost their membership. They can then go to Congress or the President and appear to have leverage with the amount of voters backing the group.



# {{< heading "Methods" >}}
Interest groups lobby in a number of ways. 
- Important sources of information to Congress members who agree with their ideas
- Help Congress members' campaigns
- Write bills and propose them to Congress members
- Guide bureaucracy in carrying out a law

Revolving door is when a lobby hires former Congress members. This is to immediately increase their access to current members of Congress and also have their expertise. Nowadays, some number of years must pass after their time in office before they become a lobbyist. 

This limitation began to emerge in the Honest Leadership and Open Government Act (2007). It also prohibited gifts. The Lobbying Disclosure Act (1995) required lobbying organizations to register with the House and Senate. This had numerous loopholes.

All of this is insider tactics. Outsider tactics are used by people without insider access. To still influence, they must resort to demonstrations.

Groups try to get public support through grassroots activity or bottom up activity. This is performing political actions that anyone can do to try to get the government to do something (e.g., letter writing). Sometimes, they employ astroturfing—creating the perception that a lot of people agree with the group's goals. Filing lawsuits about something that seems wrong will generate attention on the issue and the group itself.
