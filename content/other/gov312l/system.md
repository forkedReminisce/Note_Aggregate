The **international system** is all over and self-enforcing. Actor attributes (e.g., individual interests) and structural constraints (i.e., context and circumstance) govern activity. Additionally, the behaviors of individual actor(s) has value. Reactions, that can also be anticipated. 

The system is made up of multiple *structures* that distributes powers among its actors. Structures can differentiated by whether events are judged in a material or ideational way. Material interprets it at face value, whereas ideational takes into consideration context. For example, possession of a nuke is concerning, but this can be OK if its an ally's. Structure is also differentiated by the degree it influences its actors: natural or constitutive (i.e., the structure defines part of its actors' identity).

There are several types of actors:
- States: differentiated on national interests and capabilities
- Great powers: states possessing overwhelming economic and military power 
- Domestic groups (e.g., political parties)
- International organizations (IOs)
- Multinational corporations (MNCs)
- Transnational activists and nongovernmental organizations (NGOs): advocate for social issues
- Individuals (particularly significant ones)

## {{< heading "Iran Nuclear Accord" >}}
To look into the inner workings of the international system, consider the events surrounding the Iran Nuclear Accord. This was devised to quell Iran, who the U.S. saw as a terrorist state, from creating nuclear weapons. ==With powerful sanctions, the U.S. and other powerful states pressured Iran into agreeing.==

Anarchy is the absence of an enforcement authority. When Trump gained the presidency in 2016, he withdrew the U.S. from the Accord. Due to anarchy in the international system, there was no way to punish the U.S. Not only was the strategic dependence thrown out (i.e., the P5+1 could not carry out its promises without the U.S.), but it also convinced Iran to never enter these types of agreements ever again.



# {{< heading "Terrorism" >}}
Terrorism is the use or threatened use of violence against civilians in pursuit of some political goal. This differs from other kinds of violence because the targets are civilians, attacks are purposeful, and terrorists are non-state actors.

{{< subtext >}}
    Because terrorist groups are not states, their goals are more limited (e.g., changing the target state's foreign policy) and they have no pinpointable home base.
{{< /subtext >}}

Terrorist organizations are rational actors—exhibiting goal-oriented behavior. Targeting civilians is rational because it allows the organization to avoid confrontation with the stronger state militaries. Additionally, it can signal to their followers their resolve. Some strategies terrorist organizations use when engaging in violence are:
- Provocation: encouraging an overreaction
- Spoiling: disrupting peace negotiations to maintain a conflict between two states
- Outbidding: competing for followers 


## {{< heading "Counterterrorism" >}}
One counterterrorism technique is *defensive measures*. They are designed to prevent terrorist attacks before they take place. Domestic surveillance can violate the liberty of privacy, and increased security for potential terrorist targets adds overhead and restricts free movement of people and goods. Law enforcement is intelligence organizations investigating terrorist groups. This can result in excessive coercion of ethnic communities presumed to sympathetic to terrorist groups (e.g., coercion against Muslim Americans after 9/11). 

*Deterrence* is very hard to apply to terrorist groups because their home base is hard to find. Even if it is discovered, by the time it is, the threat would've worn off. Retaliation can also backfire by producing collateral damage that could increase local support or a worse replacement leader.

External wars take the fight directly to terrorist groups. However, this too might increase local support for terrorist groups. Wars are also politically costly domestically and internationally and take the military's attention away from other threats.



# {{< heading "International Organizations" >}}
IOs help with cooperation by addressing the collective action problem, reducing the transaction costs of coming up with a deal by providing a framework, and offer methods of enforcement. However, participating in an IOs requires states to delegate some agency. This potentially threatens sovereignty.

The United Nations is involved in peacekeeping, the protection of human rights, and more. Every member is part of the General Assembly, but the US, UK, China, Russia, France, and ten other rotating members are also in the Security Council. The named states also have veto power. Due to the size of the General Assembly, UN approval provides legitimacy for states' controversial foreign policy actions.

NATO suffers from some struggles: European shirking sees Europe not investing as much into their military as the US, and the US has had periods when it has been unwilling to accept the burden and contribute. However, NATO still offers really powerful allies to the US.



# {{< heading "Democracy" >}}
A democracy must have:
- Public Contestation: competitive elections and freedom of expression
- Inclusion: universal suffrage
- Democratic Sovereignty: the important decisions are made by elected officials

A lack of inclusion undermines the principle of democracy. Greater inclusion, therefore, creates greater legitimacy. Greater inclusion also has the effect of descriptive representation, which is that voters elect people that look like them and share their life experiences. Increased diversity then helps with legitimacy and the promotion of policies important to the community among all officials. 

{{< subtext >}}
    Diversity in government has not caught up with diversity in society.

    Inclusion also affects election outcomes.
{{< /subtext >}}

The **Democratic Peace Theory** claims that democracies are relatively unlikely to go to war with each other. However, democracies are still as likely to fight authoritarian states as authoritarian states are to each other. Elections create political costs, the international status quo is preferred by voters, and there's a shared democratic identity that encourages peaceful negotiation over military force.

However, states in a transition to democracy are war-prone. Therefore, if a number of democracies began to emerge, the world might experience a period of increased conflict. There are also alternative explanations for democratic peace: as the common enemy, Soviet Union kept Western Europe in peace during the Cold War, or the great power settlements of the Cold War maintained peace as neither alliance wanted to fight each other.

U.S. presidents historically have either tried to transform dictatorships into democracies or have U.S. democracy shine as a role model. The first tendency uses too much resources and is often unsuccessful, and the second ignores foreign threats to the point they can attack the U.S. *Good enough governance* instead calls for different approaches towards states depending on which of three groups they fall into. 
- Consolidated democracies: maintain alliances to help sustain that country's democracy
- Transitional countries: work with other democracies to nudge this country towards greater democracy 
- Despotic regimes: since democracy is probably not possible in the short-term, promote good local leaders, regardless of whether they are for democracy



# {{< heading "Globalization" >}}
**Globalization** is the integration of many national economies into a single global economy. It makes states wealthier through *comparative advantage*: specialization to minimize inefficiencies; the goods they don't make are imported from other states that specialize in them. 

However, most countries still apply trade barriers that limit globalization. Comparative advantages misses what the *Heckscher-Ohlin framework* catches: some groups will see an increase in wages while other groups' fall. The winners possess abundant factor endowments, which allows the products they serve as inputs to to get cheaper. On the other hand, the losers with scarce factor endowments are threatened by other states that have the resource in abundance. They, thus, lobby for trade barriers to protect themselves. 

*Trade deficit* (or current account deficit) is often cited for unfair trade practices by a trading partner. A trade deficit can theoretically prevent a domestic firm from employing or even cause it to lay off employees. However, reality is different; there is a **capital account** surplus that compensates as foreigners invest into the state. 

==The USD is the global reserve currency==. Not only do U.S. Treasury Bonds back central banks around the world, but they also serve as the currency of international exchange. This relies on the U.S. to run a budget deficit so that the U.S. takes loans through bonds. However, a reliance on foreign investors gives political power to them. If the investors become unconfident that the U.S. will repay the bonds, they will demand higher interest rates. The U.S. must then raise taxes.

    The USD as the global reserve currency happened over many decades of work.
{{< /subtext >}}

After WWII, Allied states met at Bretton Woods to devise methods of **economic cooperation**. In their belief, this would prevent another Nazi Germany from happening. However, economic cooperation has a commitment problem, it's hard for states to monitor compliance, and there are distribution challenges—the deal may benefit one side more. IOs address these issues in addition to conveying the economic interests of member states.

The *General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade* (GATT) hosted negotiating rounds that aimed to reduce trade barriers by everyone. However, it had no enforcement mechanism. When the *World Trade Organization* (WTO) succeeded GATT, it offered the dispute resolution mechanism. If the plaintiff wins, they get a lot of freedom and can be strategic with how they apply their retaliation. Recently, though, the WTO hasn't been able to further trade liberalization due to limited support by the larger states.

The *International Monetary Fund* (IMF) holds onto the capital it was given by member states. It serves as a lender of last resort, but only when a member faces a balance of payments crisis—since it cannot make its loan payments, lenders pull their capital out and further depreciate the currency. The IMF attaches conditionality arrangements onto the loan aimed to ensure the state is able to repay its debts in the future without IMF assistance. These conditions often stunt economic growth, but states accept them anyway because the IMF is the only body willing to lend to them. By fulfilling the conditionality arrangements, other lenders will feel confident again to invest in the state. However, since influence in the IMF is proportional to contribution to the fund, larger donors may favor particular states by relaxing the conditions for them.
