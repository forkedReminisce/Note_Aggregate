**Foreign policy** is official actions by the government (as minute as an appearance at an event) and/or statements by officials directed at some foreign audience. It is motivated by a desire to shape beliefs, capabilities, interests, and actions of foreign actors to better realize the national interests. 

{{< subtext >}}
    Actors have beliefs of other actors (including their citizens), including expectations of their future actions.
{{< /subtext >}}

National interests (e.g., democracy) come from and define the identity of Americans. The dimensions of them are: security, economic, and ideational (or normative). By how much they align with other states can lead to either cooperation or conflict. Since not every citizen will be able to agree with a set of interests, institutions like elections help decide.

The bureaucracy handles the planning and execution of national interests. These departments are:
- State Department
- Department of Defense: meets with military leaders and the Joint Chiefs of Staff
- Treasury Department: works with the Federal Reserve on currency stability and financing (e.g., borrowing)
- CIA and Directorate of National Intelligence (DNI): intelligence gathering that helps shape beliefs of world leaders about other states
- National Security Council and Advisor: coordinate the bureaucracy



# {{< heading "Grand Strategy" >}}
**Grand strategy** is the set of ideas that guide and legitimate U.S. foreign policies. Grand strategies can be compared on the following dimensions:
- International political order
- Main security threats
- National interests: geographic scope (i.e., is the U.S. concerned globally or regionally)
- Policy to achieve interests: unilateralism versus multilateralism; military actions versus diplomacy (e.g., sanctions)
- Challenges to implementing said policies


## {{< heading "Terrorism" >}}
Since 9/11, international terrorism was the main security threat. President Bush took a unilateral approach, but then President Obama saw this too costly, especially in terms of life. A multilateral approach would lessen the burden on the U.S. Despite this, Obama believed that the U.S. must lead in the world order due to its unrivaled capabilities. If the U.S. doesn't take initiative, no one will.

Alternate approaches include **isolationism** to minimize the number of adversaries and dedicate resources to within. This exploits geographic isolation, possession of nuclear weapons, and limiting foreign entanglements (e.g., participation in NATO).

**Restraint** is another approach that has recently been gaining traction, and it, like Obama, is critical of Bush's approach. Proponents argue that the U.S. has entangled in too many costly and unending conflicts and made its allies too comfortable. They want to prevent a great power war, reduce military spending and troop deployments on foreign soil, and scale back alliance obligations. However, unlike Obama, they do not believe that the U.S. is indispensable; other Western countries should be able to stand on equal ground to the U.S. 


## {{< heading "Biden Doctrine" >}}
President Biden marked a change in grand strategy when he identified that the world order was heading into an age of democracy versus autocracy. This came about for three reasons: China and Russia were catching up and disturbing democracies (e.g., cyberattacks), democracies struggled to tackle transnational problems (e.g., COVID-19), and decay of democracy from within (e.g., January 6th). The **Biden Doctrine** sought to strengthen cohesion and resilience of the democratic community against its authoritarian rivals, ensure the U.S. takes initiative in leading democracies through transnational problems, and the U.S. build a "position of strength" in the world order through reinvestment. 

President Trump returned the focus on terrorism. However, unlike the previous aim of regime change, now the belief is that immigration is the source of terrorism. In fact, he believed U.S. intervention worsened terroristic ideas in the Middle East because it radicalized.

## {{< heading "Liberal Internationalism" >}}
Liberal internationalism is a grand strategy embraces multilateralism to build a liberal international order to enhance the security and interests of the U.S. Security is seen as collective in nature, meaning that the U.S. should strive to work with its partners to combat threats (e.g., participate in NATO). Liberalism is emphasized because it would not only result in a better world, but would also create a world safer to the U.S. It also encourages military and economic intervention. Interests are global. Tends to take diplomatic means at persuading other states to align with American interests.

{{< subtext >}}
    Liberalism in this context concerns rule of law, human rights, and democracy.
{{< /subtext >}}

President Wilson pioneered liberal internationalism. The League of Nations was a collective security system that deterred future attempts to alter the territorial status quo, instead encouraging international arbitration rather than war. He also supported national self-determination (i.e., those under imperial rule should be able to decide for themselves). Of course, he also supported democracy and free trade, including open navigation of the sea (since the British dominated the seas). The challenges Wilson faced was archery in the League of Nations, and that Congress rejected joining (limits of democracy and democratic constraints in the U.S. on the President implementing his preferred foreign policy).

When Trump took his first presidency, he, and many of those he surrounded himself with, saw the liberal world order as too demanding. They instead want to go for dominance in the great-power competition by themselves. A rising China has challenged the liberal world order with its authoritarian practices. Lastly, liberal internationalism has not delivered on its promise (e.g., globalization and free trade hurt some countries' sectors).

However, this new view will result in a world that is not as open to liberal ideals. This is because the U.S. would steadily lose its influence over many governments over the world. In fact, the situation could be worse because there is a fundamental interdependence among states of the liberal world order.

This interdependence is exposed in the *problems of modernity*. Climate change, global pandemics, financial crises, etc. respect no borders; every state is as safe as the most vulnerable. The liberal world order fostered many great things around the world and protected everyone from threats.

President Roosevelt in the 1930s imagined the liberal world order. It was to save the democratic way of life and deliver a better life for all citizens around the world. He envisioned multilateral institutions that would provide a framework for international collaboration.

What would come to hurt the liberal order is that the institutions became unbundled, meaning any country can pick and choose what aspect they would like to abide by.

The U.S. can save the liberal world order by:
- Point out the successes: give leaders reasons to continue by it
- Allow states that fell behind to catch up
- Return to the original aim of Roosevelt: create an environment for collective security and protection of liberal values
- Strengthen the alliance of democracies


## {{< heading "Primacy" >}}
The grand strategy of **primacy** focuses on American hegemony, its political, economic, and military leadership in the world. Such foreign policies try to prevent the emergence of a strong rival. Interests are global. Unilateral. Supporters like to use coercion.  

President Bush is an example of primacy. He also added the idea of promoting democracy onto maintaining American hegemony. The belief was that democratic societies will align better with U.S. interests.


## {{< heading "America First" >}}
President Trump often parrots the saying "America First." His grand strategy has economic interests in the forefront, especially distributing wealth to the American worker.

Trump subscribes to neomercantilism. As a result, he has sought protectionist policies (e.g., tariffs) in order to foster domestic industry, a trade war with China and cracking down on immigration. This was a departure from open global markets of the past few administrations.

{{< subtext >}}
    Mercantilism is a old, discredited idea that aims to keep production within borders. International trade was dismissed because economic dependence on other countries is risky (e.g., COVID pandemic led to global inflation).
{{< /subtext >}}

The national security strategy was to protect homeland, promote American prosperity, preserve peace by rebuilding the military, and advance American influence abroad. All these rest on the strengthening of national sovereignty. Trump aimed to strengthen sovereignty by restricting immigration, limit participation in multilateralism and international organizations and agreements, limit overseas military commitments, substantial increases in military spending.

Trump's second term marks a significant shift in U.S. foreign policy. Gone are his previous isolationist tendencies of his first term, he has entertained the idea of territorial expansion. This is more ambitious than Bush's primacy, since no grand strategy before Trump's has considered taking over another country's territory.



# {{< heading "President" >}}
Article II of the Constitution implies presidential power over foreign policy. The Commander-in-Chief clause supposedly gives the president to use military force around the world without a congressional declaration of war. In fact, it is seen as having minimal resistance from the other branches. This article also gives the president the power to make treaties  (with Senate approval), to appoint ambassadors, and broad executive powers (to be able to claim predominance over foreign policy).

{{< subtext >}}
    Thomas Jefferson, and many presidents, have claimed that any power not explicitly given to another branch of government in the Constitution lies with the president.
{{< /subtext >}}

However, Congress does not always defer to the president in foreign policy matters. This is thanks to separation of powers and checks and balances, designed to curtail the concentration of presidential power over foreign policy. For example, Congress does have the power to declare war and to fund the military.

The national security council coordinates foreign policy agencies. This includes making sure the agencies communicate with each other, share information, and do not work at cross purposes with each other. Since these agencies are not homogenous and potentially have different goals, building influence matters to get one's voice heard. In a similar vein, agencies will devise methods to maximize the allotment of power or resources over competing agencies.

War expands presidential authority. Congress lets this happen because the president has a national constituency compared to Congress' local. This means that Congresspeople has a greater ideological concentration. Despite that, during times of crises, Congresspeople tend to move closer to the president's position. Additionally, there's Congressional uncertainty since there's the president does have intelligence they don't get.

Right after 9/11, Congress authorized the president to use military force against linked organizations. This AUMF would later be cited by President Obama in 2014 in order to use military force against ISIS, even though he declared the war on Al Qaeda three years prior.

For decades in the making, the power of the president to make foreign policy has grown, and constraints in the form of Congress, the bureaucracy, and foreign allies have all shown reluctance or declining capacity to check the president. This is thanks to polarization limiting bipartisan cooperation within Congress and a lack of a galvanizing foreign threat. 

An executive order has the force of law but not its permanence. This bypasses Congress, but Congress can pass laws to undo them (subject to Presidential veto). The Supreme Court can overturn EOs only if they're unconstitutional. 

{{< subtext >}}
    Permanence is threatened by following presidents' executive orders overwriting them.
{{< /subtext >}}
