Interest groups are a private group that want to influence a particular type of public policy (e.g., gun policies). They are lobbies, and they often have lobbyists who primary goal is to lobby—try to influence public policy by contacting public officials.

{{< subtext >}}
    Other names for interest groups are special interests, lobby groups, public interest groups, and pressure groups.
{{< /subtext >}}


## {{< heading "Theories" >}}
Disturbance theory is that groups form to counteract changes in policy that end up threatening a segment of the population (e.g., abortion).

Government can be pluralist, meaning anyone can and will access government. Elitism is where government only really pays attention to the corporations. In this U.S., this tends to vary from issue to issue.



# {{< heading "Types" >}}
A large portion of interest groups are businesses. They do this to maximize revenue. They often have professional lobbyists—employees who lobby.

{{< subtext >}}
    Temporary lobbyists exist and they're hired much like how lawyers at law firms are.
{{< /subtext >}}

Unions and Professional Organizations (e.g., American Bar Association) will often interact with legislatures. 

{{< subtext >}}
    Comity Laws are state laws that outline the guidelines to obtaining a license in that particular state.
{{< /subtext >}}

Ideological Groups focus on a perspective, while Single Issue Groups have their issue. Public Interest Groups are tax exempt charities, and they pursue public good. 



# {{< heading "Membership" >}}
Group maintenance is about getting and keeping members. Centralized groups have a headquarters and field offices all over the nation. Leadership decides the goals of the group. Confederations give more freedom to the local level, which may result in some discrepancies with the national side.

Peak associations membership is comprised of businesses, not individuals. Mass associations have due-paying members. This lends to checkbook membership, where an interest group will set their dues low in order to boost their membership. They can then go to Congress or the President and appear to have leverage with the amount of voters backing the group.



# {{< heading "Methods" >}}
Interest groups lobby. They are important sources of information to Congress members who agree with their ideas. They help their campaigns. They write bills and propose them to a number of Congress members to see if any one of them would introduce it. They guide the bureaucracy in carrying out a law.

Revolving door is when a lobby hires former Congress members. This is to immediately increase their access to current members of Congress and have their expertise. Nowadays, some number of years must pass after their time in office before they become a lobbyist. 

This limitation began to emerge in the Honest Leadership and Open Government Act (2007). It also prohibited gifts. The Lobbying Disclosure Act (1995) required lobbying organizations to register with the House and Senate. This had numerous loopholes.

All of this is insider tactics. Outsider tactics are used by people without insider access. To still influence, they must resort to demonstrations.

Groups try to get public support through grassroots activity or bottom up activity. This is performing political actions that anyone can do to try to get the government to do something (e.g., letter writing). Sometimes they employ astroturfing, which is creating the perception that a lot of people agree with the group's goals (e.g., buying followers). Filing lawsuits about something that seems wrong will generate attention on the issue and the group itself.
