Politics is the use of authority to allocate scare resources and settle conflicts. *Authority* is the capacity to direct the behavior of others. This comes in the form of **legitimate coercion**. 

Coercion is necessary to uphold the political order. Domestically, a monopoly on coercion is crucial to dispelling rebels. There are even greater implications on the international level. With the ability to threaten war, states can discourage other states from messing with them. Additionally, military power helps determine every state's place in the international system.

However, granting political organizations the capacity for coercion comes with the risk of it being used for predation. This is known as the *coercion dilemma*. Democracy solves this issue on the domestic front, while the international system requires retaliatory military force. If this military force needs to be maintained after the conflict, it can be legitimated by members recognizing a continued threat, approval through democracy, and consulting multilateral alliances.



# {{< heading "War" >}}
War is costly. Borders change and there are refugee flows, which end up altering the demographic composition of the state. More horrifically, people die and the economy gets destroyed.

The external security threats of war can create an opportunity for the government to expand. Raising taxes, expanding the bureaucracy, and heightening restrictions on liberties; citizens willingly gives these up for security. However, there's always the flip side. The longer the war goes on, the more frustrated citizens will be of the costs. This leads to a rise in opposition, which can sometimes result in revolution. 


## {{< heading "Bargaining Model Of War" >}}
The **Bargaining Model of War** is the framework that explains why war breaks out. It rests on the assumption that ==war is costly==: human costs, destruction of the infrastructure of the economy, and opportunity costs of the economy. 

All wars being with a conflict of interest. ==To preface, there is always a range of settlements that leaves both parties better off==. However, states want to maximize their part of the deal. When war offers a better deal than bargaining, then so be it. This relies on the **expected utility of war**—the likelihood of winning (based on military capabilities) minus the potential costs of going to war. When a party's concessions is less than the other party's expected utility, the other party will go to war.

**Private information** is when a party has secrets of its capabilities or resolve—willingness to pay the costs of war. This can cause the other party to have an inaccurate gauge of this party's expected utility. The party can try to outright admit their secret, but it will likely be met with skepticism. Since *costly signals* come with a price for the party, they're the best option for conveying private information. 
- Audience costs come with going public, and it is the risk of failing to deliver
- Sunk costs: subtle demonstration of power

The **commitment problem** is when a party thinks any peace deal with the other party will be broken by it demanding more concessions in the future. During this supposed time of peace, the distribution of power will shift towards the other side. Therefore, the declining side finds it logical to fight a preventative war now before it gets relatively weaker.

Institutional mechanisms can solve the commitment problem by making increased demands by the other party less attractive. Arms control agreements stabilizes distribution of power. Democracy also does through the people voting, thereby controlling military spending. Additionally, membership in international organizations can convey to the other side some set of expectations (e.g., being in NATO implies no plan to go to war).
