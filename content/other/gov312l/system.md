The **international system** is all over and self-enforcing. Actor attributes (e.g., individual interests) and structural constraints (i.e., context and circumstance) govern activity. Additionally, the behaviors of individual actor(s) has value. Reactions, that can also be anticipated. 

The system is made up of multiple *structures* that distributes powers among its actors. Structures can differentiated by whether events are judged in a material or ideational way. Material interprets it at face value, whereas ideational takes into consideration context. For example, possession of a nuke is concerning, but this can be OK if its an ally's. Structure is also differentiated by the degree it influences its actors: natural or constitutive (i.e., the structure defines part of its actors' identity).

There are several types of actors:
- States: differentiated on national interests and capabilities
- Great powers: states possessing overwhelming economic and military power 
- Domestic groups (e.g., political parties)
- International organizations (IOs)
- Multinational corporations (MNCs)
- Transnational activists and nongovernmental organizations (NGOs): advocate for social issues
- Individuals (particularly significant ones)

## {{< heading "Iran Nuclear Accord" >}}
To look into the inner workings of the international system, consider the events surrounding the Iran Nuclear Accord. This was devised to quell Iran, who the U.S. saw as a terrorist state, from creating nuclear weapons. ==With powerful sanctions, the U.S. and other powerful states pressured Iran into agreeing.==

Anarchy is the absence of an enforcement authority. When Trump gained the presidency in 2016, he withdrew the U.S. from the Accord. Due to anarchy in the international system, there was no way to punish the U.S. Not only was the strategic dependence thrown out (i.e., the P5+1 could not carry out its promises without the U.S.), but it also convinced Iran to never enter these types of agreements ever again.



# {{< heading "Terrorism" >}}
Terrorism is the use or threatened use of violence against civilians in pursuit of some political goal. This differs from other kinds of violence because the targets are civilians, attacks are purposeful, and terrorists are non-state actors.

{{< subtext >}}
    Because terrorist groups are not states, their goals are more limited (e.g., changing the target state's foreign policy) and they have no pinpointable home base.
{{< /subtext >}}

Terrorist organizations are rational actors—exhibiting goal-oriented behavior. Targeting civilians is rational because it allows the organization to avoid confrontation with the stronger state militaries. Additionally, it can signal to their followers their resolve. Some strategies terrorist organizations use when engaging in violence are:
- Provocation: encouraging an overreaction
- Spoiling: disrupting peace negotiations to maintain a conflict between two states
- Outbidding: competing for followers 


## {{< heading "Counterterrorism" >}}
One counterterrorism technique is *defensive measures*. They are designed to prevent terrorist attacks before they take place. Domestic surveillance can violate the liberty of privacy, and increased security for potential terrorist targets adds overhead and restricts free movement of people and goods. Law enforcement is intelligence organizations investigating terrorist groups. This can result in excessive coercion of ethnic communities presumed to sympathetic to terrorist groups (e.g., coercion against Muslim Americans after 9/11). 

*Deterrence* is very hard to apply to terrorist groups because their home base is hard to find. Even if it is discovered, by the time it is, the threat would've worn off. Retaliation can also backfire by producing collateral damage that could increase local support or a worse replacement leader.

External wars take the fight directly to terrorist groups. However, this too might increase local support for terrorist groups. Wars are also politically costly domestically and internationally and take the military's attention away from other threats.



# {{< heading "International Organizations" >}}
IOs help with cooperation by addressing the collective action problem, reducing the transaction costs of coming up with a deal by providing a framework, and offer methods of enforcement. However, participating in an IOs requires states to delegate some agency. This potentially threatens sovereignty.

The United Nations is involved in peacekeeping, the protection of human rights, and more. Every member is part of the General Assembly, but the US, UK, China, Russia, France, and ten other rotating members are also in the Security Council. The named states also have veto power. Due to the size of the General Assembly, UN approval provides legitimacy for states' controversial foreign policy actions.

NATO suffers from some struggles: European shirking sees Europe not investing as much into their military as the US, and the US has had periods when it has been unwilling to accept the burden and contribute. However, NATO still offers really powerful allies to the US.



# {{< heading "Democracy" >}}
The preconditions to be considered a democracy are:
- Public Contestation: competitive elections and freedom of expression
- Inclusion: Universal suffrage
- Democratic Sovereignty: the important decisions must be made by elected officials

A lack of inclusion undermines the principle of democracy. Greater inclusion, therefore, creates greater legitimacy. Greater legitimacy produces more acceptance of decisions because everyone chose the leader. Additionally, inclusion increases loyalty. Greater inclusion also has the effect of descriptive representation, which is that voters elect people that look like them and share their life experiences, increasing the diversity within the government. This helps with legitimacy and promotion of policies important to the community among all officials. However, diversity in government has not caught up to diversity in society. Inclusion also affects national election outcomes.

The **Democratic Peace Theory** claims that democracies are relatively unlikely to go to war with each other. However, democracies are still as likely to fight with authoritarian states as authoritarian states are to each other. Elections create political costs, institutional checks and balances address the commitment problem as the benefits of an international status quo can be felt at home, and there's a shared democratic identity that promotes peaceful negotiation over military force. 

However, states in a transition to democracy are war-prone. Therefore, if a number of democracies began to emerge, the world might experience a period of increased conflict. There are also alternative explanations for democratic peace: the Soviet Union was a common enemy in the Cold War that kept Western Europe in peace, or great power settlements of the Cold War maintained peace as neither alliance wanted to fight each other.

U.S. presidents have either tried to transform dictatorships into liberal democracies or demonstrate the success of U.S. democracy. The first tendency uses too much resources and is often unsuccessful, and the second ignores foreign threats so they can foster and attack the U.S. Good enough governance instead designates separate approaches towards states depending on which of three groups they fall into. 
- Consolidated democracies: maintain alliance and trade commitments to help sustain that country's democracy
- Transitional countries: work with other democracies to nudge this country towards greater democracy 
- Despotic regimes: since democracy is probably not possible in the short-term, promote good local leaders, regardless of whether they are for democracy
