A political party encapsulates a particular common set of values. As a result, a network of like-minded people gets tied to the party. This is useful to both the electorate and politicians. 



# {{< heading "History" >}}
Realignment describes a mass of people switching parties. This happens around every 40 to 60 years. This is preceded by dealignment, when people turn independent. Realignment takes place in a critical election, where people must pick a party.

{{< subtext >}}
    During dealignment, no party dominates elections.
{{< /subtext >}}

The First Party System was between the Federalists and the Anti-Federalists. The Revolution of 1800 concerns the presidential election between incumbent John Adams and Thomas Jefferson. Jefferson won and Adams peacefully transferred power. This was the first of its kind in the world. 

The Fifth Party System saw the New Deal coalition dominate support of the Democratic Party. This coalition was diverse. This era ended when President Nixon's Southern strategy sought to break up this New Deal coalition and get them to join the Republicans. People also just started to vote with their social values in mind.

Today, we're unsure whether or not the nation is under the Sixth Party System. Ticket splitting is more common, which is the opposite of straight-line voting—voting for one party down the ballot. This is reflective of a candidate-centered era rather than a party-centered era.



# {{< heading "Organization" >}}
Parties inform the electorate on how to vote, the choice to pick, why to vote that way, and more. They support candidates with money, volunteers, and more. However, this gives the party leverage over candidates (e.g., to support a particular stance). This tends to generate homogenous candidates.

Associated with the party are national committees and informal groups. National committees help get funding and figure out the seats that need a candidate of the party. Informal groups are not officially part of the party, but their opinion is valued by the party. These groups also help get the party elected. 


## {{< heading "Presidential Nominees" >}}
Every four years, each party holds their own **national party convention** to pick their presidential candidate. A big factor in this decision is the results of each state's primary or caucus. *Caucuses* are much less common because everyone must come together to publicly talk about their candidate then publicly vote. This takes forever. 

*Primaries* can be divided into categories.
- Closed: only open to people with membership to the respective party
- Open: anyone can vote, but they're only allowed at one party's
- Blanket: parties share a ballot 
- Jungle: shared ballot but the top two get to race

The results of primaries and caucuses grants a candidate pledged delegates. *Superdelegates*, on the other hand, are just very special people to the party and vote at their discretion. Due to the Republicans' confederal nature, their state parties have more freedom in how to distribute pledged delegates. Democrats are more unitary and subject all states to the Proportional System. They also have many times more superdelegates. With so much influence, superdelegates do not get to vote on the first ballot. 

{{< subtext >}}
    Multiple ballots occur when no candidate gets a majority (50% + 1) of delegates. This leads to a brokered convention, where delegates try to persuade each other.
{{< /subtext >}}

Whoever becomes the presidential nominee of the party becomes the party's administrator. They don't handle the day to day operations of the party, but they do select a national chair to do just that.

If this nominee wins and they're popular, chances are that the coat-tail effect will have occurred. That is, candidates of the same party for other offices will have won.
