Particles are a crucial part of Japanese; they mark important details to the sentence or **just serve adverbial or conjunctive functions**. The latter is the focus of this article.



# {{< heading "Lists" >}}
**と joins two *nouns* with "and."** This may be chained, but every subsequent と is replaced with a comma. Alternatively, every と is replaced with ・. Regardless of how it's written, /to/ will be pronounced every time.

"I am Japanese and a doctor" is actually short for "I am Japanese and I am a doctor." Therefore, と would not be applicable despite looking so. There are other ways to cover the other functions of the English "and," one of which being **そして**. It's used to immediately build on top of information. Think "and" at the start of a sentence or "then." 

と creates **complete lists**, suggesting that there are no *omitted* items that are satisfiable. **や**'s purpose is to create incomplete lists. When the items are incompatible, や translates to "or." や is able to do this because each entity's independence is lowered as compared to と. や also pairs really well with predicates with a wide scope or when the speaker wants to direct the listener's attention to the information that follows the list, *even when the list is complete*. Additionally, because と often introduces its entities, や conversely works old information.

{{% example_jp
    jp="「山椒魚は、杉苔や銭苔を眺めることを好まなかった。」"
    en="\"*The salamander did not care for gazing at the cedar moss or liverwort*.\""
%}}

など often follows the final noun in an incomplete list to reference the omitted-yet-still-applicable items. のような is an alternative. など can also attach to a noun or verb to reference a list that includes said item. なんか is a colloquial variant in this regard. There are also the variants なんど, なぞ, and なんぞ. Other particles will appear before these particles, except が and を do not appear at all.

**し** is like や but **for full blown clauses**. The ideas are related such that they contribute to an overarching idea. 

{{< subtext >}}
    It can attach to the polite form, but only really in spoken speech.

    し cannot suggest a chronological order; that is reserved for the て-form.
{{< /subtext >}}



# {{< heading "Conjunctions" >}}
**が** follows a clause that describes the **premise** of the conversation or **sets up a contradiction**. The clause is allowed to be the final one. Alteratively, **けれど** can also lead up to a contradiction or **just connect clauses without any nuance**. It's possible for けれど to sound more polite and indirect. 

{{< subtext >}}
    けれども is politer than けれど; けど is more casual than けれど. 
{{< /subtext >}}



# {{< heading "Emotion" >}}
A class of final particles serve to convey a particular emotion. **よ** implores the listener to take in the information. It pops up more often than an English speaker might think because the alternative is coming off as dismissive and emotionless. 

{{< subtext >}}
    Sentences with よ can rise or fall the entire way to generate particular nuances. This is a critical parameter for よ.

    よ may attach to the person that the speaker is calling out to. It's quite dramatic.
{{< /subtext >}}

**ね** is used to either seek confirmation or answer. The answer may become less direct. Only the particle has a rising intonation, but it may start earlier if the question is genuinely being asked. な is the masculine version of ね, and it should not be used in polite speech. かね softens the directness of a request. When as よね, the speaker already knows that the listener agrees and they are just affirming it. 

っと declares something with a light tone in plain speech. とさ is used to finish a story in polite speech.



# {{< heading "\"Also\"" >}}
<!-- chaining (e.g., XもYも linked to the same thing) as a term sucks. replace it.  -->
**も** marks nouns, adjectives, and adjectival nouns that **too share a quality** to something. Adjectives conjugate as ～くも and adjectival nouns as ～でも. Multiple も make that "something" each other. If there are multiple *disjoint* も in the same sentence, the linked thing can be different between them. If the noun is the topic, subject, or direct object, も absorbs those particles. But it doesn't absorb every particle.
<!-- 美人でもなく（て）かわいくもない人に一目惚れすることはあるのか。 when used with も, it seems that て can be omitted -->

{{< subtext >}}
    も softens the overall tone of the sentence.

    も can translate to "even." This is especially true with negative predicates.
{{< /subtext >}}

{{% example_jp
    jp="「**僕も**お腹が空いた。」"
    en="\"***I**'m hungry, **too***.\""
%}}

When after counter expressions, も interprets reaching (and potentially exceeding) the amount as absurd in the speaker's mind. は is similar but it isn't all dramatic about it. This gets flipped on its head with negative sentences, with the quantity now being a threshold that isn't surpassed. Of course, も implies the current situation is way short, whereas は is just stative. The dramatics can modify a noun using もの.

By attaching も to the て-form, "even" is created. It also often lessens the tone of the overall sentence. Appending いい, かまわない, or さしっかえない refer to permission, and も may be removed to be more direct. The adverb 何といっても means "no matter what they say." Finally, verbal predicates can attach literally でも to their て-form to say that the main clause (which must possess volition) should or will be carried regardless of what this clause says. 

{{< subtext >}}
    <!-- mean ～くても? -->
    Adjectival nouns use ～ても.
    
    Nouns can use でも even if the copula wouldn't typically be placed there.

    ～ないでも is uncommon.
{{< /subtext >}}



# {{< heading "Reason" >}}
**し** indicates **causes for the overarching clause**. Like it's "and" function, some reasons may be omitted. If the result comes first, し does not attach to the final clause, unless there's only one clause given. Or, in colloquial speech, the result may be omitted, so a final し allows the listener to not think about the reasons too much and instead focus on whatever the result is.

{{% example_jp
    jp="「**ちょっと用事がありますし**、お先に帰らせていただきます。」"
    en="\"***I got some chores**, so I’ll be heading home first*.\""
%}}

し can appear together with から and ので, with し marking every reason except the last, leaving it for the other particles. で is an alternate "final" particle, and it is sometimes preceded with a し of its own. のだ and んだ can also be a final particle, but if し then follows them, then the clause is denoted as the primary cause.

The clauses may alternatively be effects and the main clause is the cause. In this case, だから may appear at the start of the first result or から or ので attach onto the (preceding) overarching clause.



# {{< heading "\"Only\"" >}}
**だけ** attaches to clauses, with adjectival nouns requiring な to go in between. It may alternatively be at the sentence end to say that there is nothing more. 

{{< subtext >}}
    If the clause is the subject or direct object, the respective particles may be omitted. If they are put in, they must go after だけ.
    
    When だけ attaches to a demonstrative, the demonstrative may be interpreted as a quantity.

    At sentence end, following だけ with the copula makes the sentence more blunt. Adding のこと in between creates a forceful tone. 
{{< /subtext >}}

{{% example_jp
    jp="「テストのことを思うだけで不安になった。」"
    en="\"*I became uneasy just at the thought of the test*.\""
%}}

There are some special constructions involving だけ. だけでなく means "not only." なるべく and できるだけ may be used adverbially to mean "if possible" or "as much as possible." The former is more formal, and the latter may modify nouns with の. 

{{< subtext >}}
    だけではなく and だけでじゃなく are possible.

    なるべく can use の as なるたけ or なるだけ, but it's rare.
{{< /subtext >}}



# {{< heading "Comparison" >}}
**に** marks the **standard** that the subject gets compared to. Some unnatural standards include the topic someone is knowledgeable about, a document to be in accordance with, or an opponent or the battle itself. 

{{< subtext >}}
    For topic of knowledge, について can mean "about." 
{{< /subtext >}}

Consider an alternative: **と** performs the comparison with both objects at **equal standing**. に, instead, treats the standard as a superior to challenge. **から** may also be used for comparisons, but as a starting point marker, it **flips the direction of comparison** to be starting point versus subject. To express that the subject is **more than** the other, mark the other thing with **より**.

{{% example_jp
    jp="「**駅から**遠い。」"
    en="\"*It is far **from the train station***.\""
%}}

A "base number" can be marked with に. For example, once *a year* and one in *three people*. What follows is basically what can fit in that base number. Multipliers are valid, using the 倍 counter (e.g., four-fold in three years).
