A political party encapsulates a particular common set of values. The electorate can identify as it, there are official organizations for parties, and politician carry out the values. Candidates get Party Labels, which is informative to voters. 

{{< subtext >}}
    If a candidate really wants funding from the party, they must say they support the values of the party.
{{< /subtext >}}

Political parties provide a network of alike people. This makes it so that politicians can quickly collaborate and vote the same way. There's a side effect where figures in the party will try to mobilize voters in other places to vote for their party.



# {{< heading "History" >}}
Realignment describes a mass of people switching parties. This happens around every 40 to 60 years. This is preceded with dealignment, when people turn independent. This shifts in a critical election, where people pick a party.

{{< subtext >}}
    During dealignment, no party can be seen dominating elections.
{{< /subtext >}}

The First Party System was between the Federalists and the Anti-Federalists. The Revolution of 1800 concerns the presidential election between incumbent John Adams and Thomas Jefferson. Jefferson won, and Adams peacefully transferred power, and this was the first of its kind in the world. 

The Second Party System starts with Andrew Jackson. His spoils system meant that when his party eventually lost power, everyone basically needed to be cleared. During the Civil War, the Third Party System emerges. Party machines develop and voter manipulation begins to happen. The Progressive Era brought primaries, elections no longer handled by parties, and Australian ballots (private ballots). The Fourth Party System ends with the Great Depression.

The Fifth Party System saw the New Deal coalition dominate influence in the Democratic Party. This coalition was diverse. Nixon's Southern strategy sought to break up this New Deal coalition and get them to join the Republicans. People also just started to vote with their social values.

Today, ticket splitting is more common. This is the opposite of straight-line voting, where someone will vote for one party down the ballot. Candidate-centered era rather than party-centered era.



# {{< heading "Organization" >}}
Democrats are organized on a unitary system while Republicans are on a confederal system. This means that the Republicans have a unique state party platforms and a national one. A party platform are basically campaign promises. 

National party conventions by each party are held every four years to decide who will be their presidential candidate (taking account primaries and such). This presidential nominee is the administrator, but they select a national chair to handle the day to day operations of the party. 

National committees help get funding and figure out the seats that need a party candidate. Informal groups are not officially part of the party but their opinion is valued by the party. These groups also help get the party elected. 

Parties inform the electorate. How to vote, the choice to go, why to vote this way, and so on. They support candidates (e.g., money or volunteers), but sometimes candidates must follow orders from the party (e.g., "support this idea"). This leads to homogenous candidates. The coat-tail effect in presidential elections bolster candidates of the same party; if the presidential candidate is really popular, candidates of the same party on the down ballot will also benefit.

A candidate gets nomination through primaries or caucuses. Caucuses are run by fewer states; people come talk about their candidate and then publicly vote. This can take forever. Primaries can be divided into categories. A closed primary is only open to people with membership to the respective party. An open primary allows someone to vote at any party's primary, but only one. Blanket primaries are where the parties share a ballot and only one person may be selected for each office. Jungle primaries also have a shared ballot and the top two go to the general election. Ranked choice has someone rank the candidates. If someone is ranked first by 50% of people, they are the candidate. If not, the person ranked first by the least amount of people is pulled off and second place becomes first. Keep going until the condition is met.

A presidential candidate must win a certain amount of primaries and caucuses. This number is different between the parties. On the Republican side, the state parties have more freedom (e.g., how to distribute delegates) over their state's primary or caucus. The Democrats have the Democratic National Committee set the rules for all the states. It's a Proportional System, and to get at least one delegate from a state, the minimum threshold of 15% from the primary/caucus must be reached. They have 10 times the number of superdelegates compared to Republicans. Because of this influence, they do not vote on the first ballot.
