The **international system** is all over and self-enforcing. Actor attributes (e.g., individual interests) and structural constraints (i.e., context and circumstance) govern activity. Additionally, the behaviors of individual actor(s) has value. Reactions, that can also be anticipated. 

The system is made up of multiple *structures* that distributes powers among its actors. Structures can differentiated by whether events are judged in a material or ideational way. Material interprets it at face value, whereas ideational takes into consideration context. For example, possession of a nuke is concerning, but this can be OK if its an ally's. Structure is also differentiated by the degree it influences its actors: natural or constitutive (i.e., the structure defines part of its actors' identity).

There are several types of actors:
- States: differentiated on national interests and capabilities
- Great powers: states possessing overwhelming economic and military power 
- Domestic groups (e.g., political parties)
- International organizations (IOs)
- Multinational corporations (MNCs)
- Transnational activists and nongovernmental organizations (NGOs): advocate for social issues
- Individuals (particularly significant ones)

    Great powers can assume a disproportionate role in international politics thanks to their capabilities.

    MNCs help transfer capital and technology from wealthy economies to poorer ones. 

    MNCs can use pressure to have significant political influence separate from national governments.
{{< /subtext >}}



## {{< heading "Iran Nuclear Accord" >}}
To look into the inner workings of the international system, consider the events surrounding the Iran Nuclear Accord. This was devised to quell Iran, who the U.S. saw as a terrorist state, from creating nuclear weapons. ==With powerful sanctions, the U.S. and other powerful states pressured Iran into agreeing.==

Anarchy is the absence of an enforcement authority. When Trump gained the presidency in 2016, he withdrew the U.S. from the Accord. Due to anarchy in the international system, there was no way to punish the U.S. Not only was the strategic dependence thrown out (i.e., the P5+1 could not carry out its promises without the U.S.), but it also convinced Iran to never enter these types of agreements ever again.



# {{< heading "Terrorism" >}}
Terrorism is the use or threatened use of violence against civilians in pursuit of some political goal. This differs from other kinds of violence because civilians are targets, attacks are not random and are a step towards political goals, and that they are non-state actors. 

{{< subtext >}}
    Because terrorist groups are not states, their goals are more limited (e.g., changing the target state's foreign policy) and they have no home base.
{{< /subtext >}}

Terrorist organizations are rational actors—exhibiting goal-oriented behavior. Targeting civilians is rational because it allows the organization to avoid confrontation with the stronger state militaries. Additionally, it can signal to their followers their resolve.

Some strategies terrorist organizations use when engaging in political violence is:
- Provocation: prompting an overreaction
- Spoiling: disrupting peace negotiations 
- Outbidding: competing for followers with violence


## {{< heading "Counterterroism" >}}
Defensive measures are designed to prevent terrorist attacks before they take place. Domestic surveillance (e.g., the Patriot Act) potentially violate the liberty of privacy. Law enforcement is domestic intelligence monitor and infiltrate terrorist groups and disrupt their operations. However, this can lead to excessive coercion and targeting of ethnic communities presumed to sympathetic to terrorist groups (e.g., coercion against Muslim Americans after 9/11). Increased security for potential terrorist targets comes with extra cost and restricts free movement of people and goods.

Deterrence is threats of retaliation. However, deterrence is very hard to apply to terrorist groups because they don't have a central headquarters. Even if they did, by the time the base is located, the threat would've worn off. Retaliation can also backfire by producing collateral damage that could increase local support or killing off a leader leads to the replacement being worse.

External wars take the fight to terrorist groups, with the ultimate goal of destroying the terrorist networks. However, this might increase local support for terrorist groups. Wars are also politically costly domestically and internationally. They also take the military's attention away from other threats.



# {{< heading "International Organizations" >}}
IOs facilitate cooperation. Cooperation requires concessions from all states, and there often is minimal repercussions for violating the terms. IOs help minimize the collective action problem, reduce the transaction costs of coming up with a deal by providing a framework, and offer a path for enforcement. 

However, participating in an IOs requires states to delegate some agency for certain policy. This potentially threatens sovereignty and policymaking. 

The United Nations is involved in peacekeeping, the protection of human rights, and more. When states are trying to get international support for a foreign policy action, they appeal to the UN. It ie comprised of the General Assembly (everyone) and the Security Council (US, UK, China, Russia, France with veto power and ten rotating members). Even though it seems that the UN does nothing, its vast membership provides legitimacy for controversial policies for states if they get UN approval. 

NATO suffered from some struggles. European shirking was that the European states were not investing much into their military as much as the US. The US has also had periods when it has been unwilling to contribute in response to the burden. However, NATO still offers really powerful allies to the US.
