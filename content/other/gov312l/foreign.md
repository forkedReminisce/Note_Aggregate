**Foreign policy** is official actions by the government (as minute as an appearance at an event) and/or statements by officials directed at some foreign audience. It is motivated by a desire to shape beliefs, capabilities, interests, and actions of foreign actors to better realize the national interests. 

{{< subtext >}}
    Actors have beliefs of other actors (including their citizens), including expectations of their future actions.
{{< /subtext >}}

National interests (e.g., democracy) come from and define the identity of Americans. The dimensions of them are: security, economic, and ideational (or normative). By how much they align with other states can lead to either cooperation or conflict. Since not every citizen will be able to agree with a set of interests, institutions like elections help decide.

The bureaucracy handles the planning and execution of national interests. These departments are:
- State Department
- Department of Defense: meets with military leaders and the Joint Chiefs of Staff
- Treasury Department: works with the Federal Reserve on currency stability and financing (e.g., borrowing)
- CIA and Directorate of National Intelligence (DNI): intelligence gathering that helps shape beliefs of world leaders about other states
- National Security Council and Advisor: coordinate the bureaucracy



# {{< heading "Grand Strategy" >}}
**Grand strategy** is the set of ideas that guide and legitimate U.S. foreign policies. Grand strategies can be compared on the following dimensions:
- International political order
- Main security threats
- National interests: geographic scope (i.e., is the U.S. concerned globally or regionally)
- Policy to achieve interests: unilateralism versus multilateralism; military actions versus diplomacy (e.g., sanctions)
- Challenges to implementing said policies


## {{< heading "Primacy" >}}
The grand strategy of **primacy** focuses on American hegemony, its political, economic, and military leadership in the world. Such foreign policies try to prevent the emergence of a strong rival, potentially relying on coercion to do so. Interests are global and unilateralism is preferred. 

President Bush is an example of primacy. He also added on top of it the idea of promoting democracy. The motivation was that democratic societies would get in line with the U.S.


## {{< heading "Terrorism" >}}
Since 9/11, international terrorism was the main security threat. President Bush took a unilateral approach, but then President Obama saw this too costly, especially in terms of life. A multilateral approach would lessen the burden on the U.S. Despite this, Obama believed that the U.S. must lead in the world order due to its unrivaled capabilities. If the U.S. doesn't take initiative, no one will.

Alternate approaches include **isolationism** to minimize the number of adversaries and dedicate resources to within. This exploits geographic isolation, possession of nuclear weapons, and limiting foreign entanglements (e.g., participation in NATO).

**Restraint** is another approach that has recently been gaining traction, and it, like Obama, is critical of Bush's approach. Proponents argue that the U.S. has entangled in too many costly and unending conflicts and made its allies too comfortable. They want to prevent a great power war, reduce military spending and troop deployments on foreign soil, and scale back alliance obligations. However, unlike Obama, they do not believe that the U.S. is indispensable; other Western countries should be able to stand on equal ground to the U.S. 


## {{< heading "Biden Doctrine" >}}
President Biden marked a change in grand strategy when he identified that the world order was heading into an age of democracy versus autocracy. This came about for three reasons: China and Russia were catching up and disturbing democracies (e.g., cyberattacks), democracies struggled to tackle transnational problems (e.g., COVID-19), and decay of democracy from within (e.g., January 6th). The *Biden Doctrine* sought to strengthen cohesion and resilience of the democratic community against its authoritarian rivals, ensure the U.S. takes initiative in leading democracies through transnational problems, and the U.S. build a "position of strength" in the world order through reinvestment. 

Biden is best described as a **liberal internationalist**, meaning he embraces multilateralism to build a liberal international order. On the key points of differentiation, interests are global and diplomacy is preferred over force. Liberalism is so valued, especially by President Roosevelt, because it is believed it would lead in a better world in addition to creating a world safer to the U.S.

President Wilson was the one who pioneered liberal internationalism. He advocated for the League of Nations, democracy, and free trade. However, the League of Nations suffered from archery, not to mention the fact that Congress rejected joining.

President Trump weakened the liberal order. Additionally, China has shown that authoritarianism can work and liberal internationalism simply has not delivered on its promise (e.g., globalization and free trade hurt some countries' sectors). 

As the U.S. enters great-power competition in a unilateral fashion, the world will become less open to liberal ideals.  This is because the U.S. would steadily lose its influence over many governments. In fact, the situation could get worse because there is a fundamental interdependence among states of the liberal world order. This interdependence is exposed in the *problems of modernity*. Climate change, global pandemics, financial crises, etc. respect no borders.

However, the U.S. can still save the liberal world order by:
- Pointing out the successes: give leaders reasons to continue by it
- Allow states that fell behind to catch up
- Return to the original aim of Roosevelt: create an environment for collective security and protection of liberal values
- Strengthen the alliance of democracies


## {{< heading "America First" >}}
President Trump often parrots the saying "America First." First, neomercantilism in the form of protectionist policies (e.g., tariffs) and a trade war with China would foster domestic industry. This was a departure from the open global markets of past administrations.

{{< subtext >}}
    Mercantilism is a old, discredited idea that aims to minimize international trade because it is risky (e.g., COVID pandemic led to global inflation).
{{< /subtext >}}

The national security strategy was to protect homeland, promote American prosperity, rebuild the military, and advance American influence abroad. All these rest on the strengthening of national sovereignty. Trump aimed to do this by restricting immigration, limit participation in multilateralism, limit overseas military commitments, and substantial increases in military spending.

{{< subtext >}}
    Trump believes that immigration is the source of terrorism. As a side note, he believes that U.S. intervention by Bush and Obama radicalized terrorism in the Middle East.
{{< /subtext >}}

Trump's second term marks a significant shift in U.S. foreign policy. Gone are his previous isolationist tendencies of his first term; he has entertained the idea of territorial expansion. No grand strategy before has ever considered this.



# {{< heading "President" >}}
Article II of the Constitution implies presidential power over foreign policy. It gives the president the power to make treaties (with Senate approval), to appoint ambassadors, and broad executive powers. The Commander-in-Chief clause in particular supposedly gives the president the power to use military force without a congressional declaration of war. In fact, there is minimal resistance from the other branches.

{{< subtext >}}
    Thomas Jefferson, and many presidents, have claimed that any power not explicitly given to another branch of government in the Constitution lies with the president.
{{< /subtext >}}

War expands presidential authority. Congress lets this happen because the president has a national constituency compared to Congress' local. This means that Congresspeople have a greater ideological concentration. Despite that, during times of crises, Congresspeople tend to align closer to the president. Additionally, there's Congressional uncertainty since the president has intelligence they don't get.

For decades in the making, the power of the president to make foreign policy has grown, and constraints in the form of Congress, the bureaucracy, and foreign allies have all shown reluctance or declining capacity to check the president. This is thanks to polarization limiting bipartisan cooperation within Congress and a lack of a galvanizing foreign threat. 

However, Congress does not always lay backwards to the president. This is thanks to the separation of powers and checks and balances. For example, Congress does have the power to declare war and to fund the military. Additionally, they can pass laws to undo executive orders, though it is subject to presidential veto. Likewise, the Supreme Court can overturn unconstitutional EOs. 

{{< subtext >}}
    EOs have the force of law but not its permanence. Its permanence is threatened by following presidents' executive orders overriding them.
{{< /subtext >}}

The national security council coordinates foreign policy agencies. This includes making sure the agencies communicate with each other, share information, and do not work at cross purposes with each other. Since these agencies are not homogenous and potentially have different goals, building influence matters to get one's voice heard. In a similar vein, agencies will devise methods to maximize the allotment of power or resources over competing agencies.
