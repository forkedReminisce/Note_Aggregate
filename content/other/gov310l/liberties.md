**Civil Liberties** are freedoms protected from governmental interference. The greatest examples are those protected by the Bill of Rights. Even if the Constitution doesn't exhaustive cover all the civil liberties, the Ninth Amendment ensures those not explicitly mentioned are still protected.

*Barron v. Baltimore* arose because a man's property was taken by the state without due process (imminent domain clause of the Fifth Amendment). It was ruled that the states did not have to follow such protections. This changed with the 14th Amendment. 

The 14th Amendment didn't automatically force states to respect protections. It was a gradual process through **Selective Incorporation**. Particular civil liberties had to be sued over in order to put the states in line.



# {{< heading "First Amendment" >}}
One of the first examples of selective incorporation was *Gitlow v. New York*, which ruled that states cloud not abridge free speech. 

*Schenck v. United States* stripped protection of free speech that can cause a danger. This ruling was motivated by the Italian Hall Disaster, where someone untruthfully yelled, "Fire!" and indirectly killed many children. ==There's some more cases that unprotects certain types of speech:==
- *Fighting Words*: speech that is provocative
- *Brandenburg v. Ohio*: direct incitement—encouraging illegal action

Prior Restrain allows the government to prohibit media from publishing certain materials. Commercial speech can be regulated if it is illegal, misleading, or there is a good reason. *Snyder v. Phelps* ruled that offensive speech is protected as long as it was expressed legally.

There may be times when obscenity is not allowed. *Miller v. California* established the Miller test to determine if the content can be classified as obscenity.

Freedom of religion is provided by two clauses. The Free Exercise Clause prohibits the government from interfering with the practice of religion. The Establishment Clause prohibits any hint of an official religion in any setting. *Lemon v. Kurtzman* established the Lemon Test to determine whether a law respects the Establishment Clause. However, the courts no longer use this test and instead just determine in the moment. 



# {{< heading "Other Amendments" >}}
The Second Amendment is the right to bear arms. *U.S. v. Miller* ruled that it only applied to military-type weapons. It got incorporated in *District of Columbia v. Heller* and *McDonald v. Chicago*, which were the results of insane restrictions on gun ownership. 

{{< subtext >}}
    Restrictions are not necessarily unconstitutional, though.
{{< /subtext >}}

The Fourth Amendment is the right against unreasonable searches. It was incorporated in *Mapp v. Ohio*. It also established the exclusionary rule, which prohibits police and prosecutors from using evidence obtained in an illegal search. 

{{< subtext >}}
    Not every unwarranted search is illegal as long as there is probable cause. For example, eventual discovery is when evidence is discovered by chance. 
    
    The Open Fields Doctrine allows observations of one's private open field to be valid evidence.
{{< /subtext >}}

The Fifth Amendment includes the right against self-incrimination and prohibits double jeopardy. *Miranda v. Arizona* introduced the idea of the Miranda rights and that suspects must be aware of them. In a similar fashion, the Sixth Amendment grants rights to the accused in criminal prosecutions.

{{< subtext >}}
    Even if evidence proving guilt is discovered after the fact, the suspect may not be tried again.
{{< /subtext >}}

The Eighth Amendment protects against cruel and unusual punishment. The death penalty was in a gray area for a while; it is not cruel and unusual as long as the method of execution wasn't originally designed for torture. *Furman v. Georgia* struck down Georgia's death penalty because the conditions to receive it were unfair. When Georgia rewrote it, *Gregg v. Georgia* upheld the new death penalty. *Ring v. Arizona* then ruled that only juries can issue the death penalty. 

Privacy is not mentioned in the Constitution, but it is a penumbral—judicially created right. *Griswold v. Connecticut* established the idea of marital privacy, meaning that married couples have a right to private communication. *Roe v. Wade* outlined the right to abortion across three stages of pregnancy. *Webster v. Reproductive Health Services* would update the guideline, but *Planned Parenthood of Southeastern Pennsylvania v. Casey* abandoned it entirely. Instead, abortion regulations are judged based on whether or not they impose an undue burden upon pregnant women. *Dobbs v. Jackson Women's Health Organization* then overruled everything by making abortion a state issue.
