In other countries, there is a single election for picking a party. The positions are then filled according to the results. In the United States, every available office is individually put up for election. Another difference is that U.S. elections are on predictable days. 

At founding, only white landowning males could vote. Universal white male suffrage came in the Jacksonian era. The 15th Amendment allowed voting regardless of race, and the 19th Amendment removed the basis on gender. 



# {{< heading "Participation" >}}
People with more education are more likely to vote. Likewise, older people are likely to vote. This is because younger people tend to be busier and growing more concerned about taxes. However, by the age of 60, voting tends to drop. Likely because they can't find transportation (to the polls or to renew their license).

Southern states are slightly less likely to vote. This is because they tend to have non-partisan ballots, removing the party cue of each candidate. Minorities are less likely to vote. This is probably because they are turned off by the fact that there is not somebody who looks like them on the ballot. 

People with strong community ties (e.g., married) are more likely to vote because policies concern them more. People who are strongly partisan are moe likely to vote. Wealth is positively correlated with the chance to vote. More women tend to vote because they live longer. 

Participation is not only voting. 
- Spectator activities: (passively) promoting a candidate (e.g., a pin)
- Trying to personally convince people to vote a certain way
- Contacting a public official
- Candidate donation
- Volunteering in a political campaign
- Climbing the ranks and taking office

Get out the Vote (GOTV) or the ground game is encouraging people to vote. Direct mobilization is reaching out to people to a personal level. Indirect mobilization is getting people to encourage their peers.


## {{< heading "Low Turnout" >}}
A reason why Americans don't vote as much as those in other countries is that they have to manually register to vote and it must match the area they reside in. Additionally, there's voter fatigue due to the sheer number of elections.

In general, people are too busy. It doesn't help that many polls are only open during the working hours of, typically, Tuesdays. That's lost pay. They also could just not like any of the candidates.

{{< subtext >}}
    The placement of voting locations can be weaponized to a particular candidate's benefit.

    Some areas implement closed polling, which assigns voters to a particular voting location that is the only place they may vote.
{{< /subtext >}}


# {{< heading "Candidates" >}}
Party affiliation sets the baseline for the number of votes one can get. From there, incumbency is a huge boost. Not only is there uncertainty associated with the running, but there's also the benefit of casework. Without incumbency, it's an uphill battle. For starters, having appealing characteristics (e.g., good looking) is beneficial.

Candidates can try to connect with the public. Or perform well in debates, which is especially impactful for underdogs. They can use Negative Campaigning, which is smearing the opponent. Insults tend to stick in people's heads. 

{{< subtext >}}
    Negative Campaigning exploits the fact that most people don't care and simple and repetitive messages work.
{{< /subtext >}}

Prospective Voting is voting for someone based on their promises. The problem is that it's really easy to say stuff but hard to execute because of the other branches. Retrospective Voting is when a candidate is judged by their past performance, even if they hadn't held that particular office or was simply associated with another administration. This leads to officials devising policies that are popular short-term but bad long-term.

Some people are single issue voters. This means they vote for the candidate that align with their views on that issue, regardless of how that candidate thinks of other issues. 


## {{< heading "Financing Campaigns" >}}
It wasn't until the last 50 years that regulations were put into place. The Federal Campaign Act (FECA) put a cap on how much one could donate. However, PACs, Political Action Committees, have a higher cap. The Federal Election Commission made it easier to form PACs. 

{{< subtext >}}
    SuperPACs can raise and spend unlimited amounts of money for support or opposing political candidates. They can directly attack a political candidate. They cannot directly coordinate with a candidate or their party, but the line is very fuzzy.
{{< /subtext >}}

Parties often coordinate spending. For example, they may make a candidate take a particular stance in order to gain funding. Alternatively, a candidate may engage in independent spending. Candidates here will spend their own money. 

There are two types of donations. Hard money is used directly for the campaign. However, soft money is used for registration, education, and mobilization of the people. This money is unregulated and is now outlawed. 

However, 527s can perform these acts as long as they don't coordinate and explicitly endorse with the candidate. Additionally, the people behind the operation can stay anonymous because they don't have to report to the IRS as long as they've already done so at the state or local level.

Similar types of tax-exempt groups offer anonymity and a soft money workaround. 501 (c) (3) cannot engage in political activity, but can with some voter registration. 501 (c) (4) can engage in political activity as long as it's not its main purpose. It can lobby for legislation, participate in campaigns, and donors do not need to be publicly disclosed. 

{{< subtext >}}
    A lot of 501 (c) (4) are not truly for political purposes. The ones that are, though, will tell the IRS that they will wind down after the election cycle. They will then bankrupt after the election cycle.
{{< /subtext >}}

Supreme Court case Citizens United (2010) removed restrictions on donations by any organization. This potentially opened the door for individuals to donate under the guise of a corporation. Additionally, there emerged another way of donating. The oldest way is donating directly to the candidate, which is under restrictions and must be publicly disclosed. This new way is sending money to another entity officially-unaffiliated with the campaign and party, which makes the money not under the same level of scrutiny. 

Money can be self-reinforcing. As in, people want to make smart choices with their money. Therefore, people will donate to candidates they think will have a good shot at winning.

Senatorial candidates tend to spend more money on TV ads. This is because their role covers the entire state. House of Representatives candidates use more targeted advertising because they will represent a congressional district. 



# {{< heading "Electoral College" >}}
The Electoral College encourages campaigning primarily in only swing states because they have a sizable portion of electoral votes that belong to neither party. Things are further compounded considering almost all states operate on a winner take all system. Nebraska is the exception, giving each congressional district a separate vote and handing the two Senator votes by popular vote in the state.

In Texas, each political party select a number of Electors that is equal to the number of electoral votes of the state. After all states make final decisions for who will get what electoral votes, Texas will send the slate of Electors of the winning party to the state capital and they will sign forms of certification. 

Because a candidate must get a majority of all the electoral votes, if this isn't hit, Congress holds an internal vote. The House decides the president, and each state, not each member, gets only one vote. The Senate decides the vice president, and each senator gets one vote.
