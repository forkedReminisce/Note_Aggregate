The civil service system is merit-based. It has applicants take a test, and their roles are assigned based on their skills. Employees (not appointed) are referred to as civil servants. The *General Schedule Rating* (G# or GS#) is one government civil service employment. The number describes the pay grades (higher is more). *Senior Executive Service* (SES) is reserved for the employees at the top that do more managerial tasks rather than what their office actually does. This allows them to be moved across offices. The Hatch Act prohibits civil servants during work hours from playing favorites depending on their political beliefs.



# {{< heading "History" >}}
Until about 100 years ago, U.S. bureaucracy selected people on the patronage system. It started with white upper class elites, but the presidency of Andrew Jackson saw him give the jobs to those loyal to him (spoils system).  

Reform happens in response to President Garfield's assassination. The assassin was someone who felt they were a core player in Garfield's win, and that they deserved a place in bureaucracy. The *Pendleton Act* or *Civil Service Reform Act* brought a merit-based system.

Into the 20th century, people were getting tired of the lack of regulation in the market. Sherman Antitrust Act, Federal Reserve Act, Pure Food and drug Act, Meat Inspection Act. The New Deal (30s) and Great Society (60s) expanded government. At this point, the government can be described as the regulatory state—the government's job is to regulate and theres a lot of bureaucracy to carry out that regulation.

Bureaucrats make regulations. Congress typically passes a law that lays out the idea, and the bureaucrats come up with the details and how to actually implement them. When the government makes a rule change, it will put it in Notice and Comment for the public to raise concerns and simply discuss the righteousness.

Street level bureaucrats are who the people personally interact with the most. Managing people in government or or contractors. 

Research and development. This could take place in government labs and facilities or contracts to universities. 



# {{< heading "Organization" >}}
The president is the top of the bureaucracy. What follows next are on the same level. The departments are represented in the Cabinet. The departments have bureaus and agencies. Independent Executive Agencies report directly to the president instead of a department, indirectly giving them more flexibility and authority. Quasi-Governmental Agencies are the private publics; the government's involved, but don't directly control it. Independent regulatory commissions acts in the people's best interest and doesn't listen to the president or Congress. Foundations are protected from political interference because they investigate ideas that really need it.

Government corporations are just federal companies (e.g., USPS) that do things that private companies cannot do or will not do. They can sell stock, reinvest, and borrow money, something other parts of government can't do. 

Congressional oversight is a way to ensure tings are being done correctly. Mandatory reports achieve the same goal. Other ways Congress can control the bureaucracy with the power of the purse—control where and how much money goes to who. GAO are the accountants for Congress, making sure the money sent is being spent (receipts) and cost-benefit analysis (is this the most effective way to dot his thing). Inspectors general.

The president can reorganize bureaucracy with congressional approval. SES. Appointment. Strategic budget proposals can allow the president to direct how funding will be divided.

Lawsuits allow the constitutionality of a rule or law to be confirmed.
