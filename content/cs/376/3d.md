Single-view ambiguity describes how a point on a 2D image could have been projected from an infinite number of 3D points on the ray. A good way of finding the exact 3D point is stereo: triangulating with two cameras and finding where the rays intersect. Alternatively, move. Pictorial cues like shading, perspective effects, and familiar objects are another strategy. These make up some of the common geometry problems:
- Calibration: figure out intrinsic properties of the camera, which can be used to derive rays
- Recovering structure
- Stereo or Epipolar geometry: given two cameras, find the original point
- Motion: figure out the correspondences between cameras



# {{< heading "Calibration" >}}
The perspective model follows:

\[
    p \equiv 
    \begin{bmatrix}
    f & 0 & u_0 \\
    0 & f & v_0 \\
    0 & 0 & 1
    \end{bmatrix}
    \begin{bmatrix}
    R_{3 \times 3} & t_{3 \times 1}
    \end{bmatrix}
    X_{4 \times 1}
\]

\(h_{\scriptstyle 0}\)

\(p\) is the 2D projection of the 3D point \(X\). \(R\) is the rotation matrix and \(t\) is the translation matrix. The \(3 \times 3\) matrix is the camera intrinsics, where \(u_0\) and \(v_0\) describe the principal point for where the origin is located. 

This expression can be alternatively written as \(p \equiv M_{3 \times 4}X\). Through a process similar to homography fitting, \(M\) can be solved for using least squares. QR decomposition can then be used on \(M\) to find \(K\), \(R\), and \(t\). 

{{< subtext >}}
    Optimizing is preferred when the least squares is overconstrained. A neural network can be initialized from the \(M\) found through least squares. 
{{< /subtext >}}

Parallel lines in a 3D space can no longer be parallel in the 2D space. Where they converge is a vanishing point. A line between vanishing points on the same plane is a vanishing line.

Therefore, there is a need for an projective invariant—a quantity that does not change under projective transformations. A good one is the cross-ratio of four points, along one line, distances to one another. This cross-ratio is the same regardless of whether the 3D points or their 2D projections are used. This allows one to compute distances between 3D points (e.g., height).

{{< subtext >}}
    The fourth point may be at infinity in the 3D space. Its projected point may be off the image.
{{< /subtext >}}



# {{< heading "Stereo Geometry" >}}
The baseline is the line drawn between thw two cameras' origins. Where the baseline intersects with the image planes are the epipoles. If these cameras capture the same 3D point, the plane formed between the baseline and projection rays is the epipolar plane. 

Epipolar lines connect the projected point to the epipole. If only one epipolar line is given, it's possible to find the projection point and its epipolar line on the other camera. This relies on the property that the 3D points along the known projection ray directly project along the second epipolar line. Another use of this property is that a pair of the epipolar lines can find correspondences of 2D projections.

{{< subtext >}}
    If the image planes are parallel, the epipoles will be to infinity and the epipolar lines will be parallel.
{{< /subtext >}}

Before delving into the algorithm for finding these correspondences, it's worth defining some variables. If the intrinsic and extrinsic parameters of one camera is known, its projection matrix is \(M = K[I, 0]\). The projection matrix of the other camera, specifically its translation matrix, is relative to this first camera. 

<!-- prime represents the second camera -->
\(K^{-1}p\) gets the projection ray as a normalized coordinate \(\hat{p}\). Let \(t_x\) be a \(3 \times 3\) recomposed translation matrix. The essential matrix is \(E = t_x R\). 
\[
    \hat{p}\prime^T E \hat{p} = 0
\]
\(\hat{p}\prime^T E\) is the epipolar line on the image with \(p\). Therefore, this checks whether the two projected points lie on each other's epipolar lines. \(E\) does need to be estimated, and \(E\) can be uniquely decomposed to \(t\) and \(R\).

Without \(K\), it's not possible to find \(\hat{p}\). Therefore, fundamental matrix is \(F = K\prime^{-T} E K^{-1}\). 
\[
    p\prime^T F p = 0
\]
This is the general method. \(F\) does not have a unique decomposition. Estimate \(F\), known as weak calibration, with eight correspondences of \(p\) and \(p\prime\)—obtained through a process similar to stitching (e.g., SIFT).

{{< subtext >}}
    \(F\) has seven degrees of freedom.
{{< /subtext >}}
