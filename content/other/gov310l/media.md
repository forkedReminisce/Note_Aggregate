# {{< heading "Public Opinion" >}}
Public opinion is the citizens' opinions on politics and government actions. Since questioning every single person is unfeasible, scientific polling is employed. A population is the group of people a researcher wants to study. A sample is the subset of the population that serves to represent the population.

{{< subtext >}}
    Polling didn't really become structured and methodological until the 60s.

    The "magic sample size" is 1000 to 1500.
{{< /subtext >}}

Random sampling gives equal chance of selection to every member of the population. When the researchers go to extrapolate the data, the results will be pretty accurate. Sampling error is the ± on percentages. Sampling bias is the sample doesn't precisely represent the population. For example, oversampling a subset or asking the questions at the wrong time when the participant's answer is uncertain. Measurement error is when the question is misinterpreted (misheard). 

{{< subtext >}}
    A straw poll greatly lacks random sampling. When this data is extrapolated, the results can be very biased. 

    Statistical dead heat is when the sampling error introduces a toss-up in the results and makes it impossible to make a conclusion.

    Push-polling is when a particular result is achieved by painting the opposition in a bad light.
{{< /subtext >}}

Framing is how information is presented, and it can shape opinions. Wording, question order (the first question sets mood), and whether it's open-ended or closed-ended (misses nuances). 

If we don't really care about a topic, we tend to give latent opinions. Alternatively, we follow the opinions of our opinion leaders—people we think know their stuff. Since people like to take cognitive shortcuts, politicians will overly simply issues (e.g., "abortion is bad"). Politician socialization is how a person's opinions are shaped by other people and their surroundings. Political ideology is consistent. Ideological polarization describes a developing hostility as more people become less moderate and more conservative or liberal. Partisanship serves as a voting cue and a source of personal identity. It also introduces confirmation bias towards media.

{{< /subtext >}}

Aggregate Public Opinion is the sum of all individual opinions. Extremes tend to get muted if there's enough randomness in the sampling. Policy mood are the issues the people are actively concerned about and how much they want the government to step up to address it.



# {{< heading "Media" >}}
Mass media are organizations that widely distribute information (doesn't include going person to person). We have free media thanks to the First Amendment, but there are laws against blatantly lying. Since media covers politics, how they frame politicians and issues really matter. They can shape viewers' opinions this way. The Communications Act of 1934 had the *Equal Time Provision*, which if a political candidate buys air time, their competitors must be offered the same time for the same price. 

{{< subtext >}}
    The Equal Time Provision is suspended during debates.
{{< /subtext >}}

In 1949, the Fairness Doctrine required that that media had to give air time to both sides of an issue. However, issues with a unanimous opinion led media to not touch certain topics. This led to the end of the doctrine in 1987. What happened next was the rise of programs that leaned heavily conservative or liberal.

Most of our media is consolidated by a few big companies. This puts into question what the media is hiding. Local stations are also more likely to have the big company do their work. However, smaller stations can survive under the umbrella when they otherwise would've died.

Media, especially trusted stations, influence a citizen's opinions on a topic. On the opposite spectrum, disliked stations can say something neutral, and a citizen will still perceive it negatively. Filtering describes what the media choose to report. Priming is where the media pedals an opinion so much that the viewer begins to share the same opinion. Framing. Slant takes priming and framing and describes the opinion that is influencing media decisions.
