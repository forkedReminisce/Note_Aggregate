# {{< heading "Race" >}}
Slave importation was banned by the Constitution because it was believed that it would eventually wither away. 

The Missouri Compromise admitted Missouri as a slave state and Maine as a free state and set a line for future slave and free states. This was to maintain the power balance between slave states and free states in regards to the Electoral College (both states had similar population sizes). 

Because slaves were escaping to free states to gain freedom, the Fugitive Slave Act forced officers in the North to detain anyone they suspected to be a runaway slave. Dred Scott v. Sanford saw a runaway slave declare their freedom. It was ruled that all blacks, free or not free, were not citizens. Therefore, slaves were private property. Congress could not regulate slavery and that it should be up to the states.

After the Civil War, there was the 13th, 14th, and 15th Amendments. Because the 14th Amendment made all former slaves citizens, the 3/5th Compromise becomes void and the Southern states gain a number of representatives. However, they do not want these formers slaves voting. 

Because elections were not run by the government at this time, Southern legislatures created black codes to prevent black people from voting among a number of things (e.g., owning property). The 14th Amendment would crack down on this by prohibiting restrictions that offer no way for a black person to possibly vote. It also banned those who helped the Confederacy from holding an office in U.S. government unless both chambers of Congress votes 2/3rds.

{{< subtext >}}
    The 14th Amendment does require that everyone be **equally protected under the law**.
{{< /subtext >}}

United States v. Cruikshank concerns a law that prohibits people from preventing others (especially blacks) from voting, a liberty. The national government went after individuals, and that was ruled against. Instead, the national government had to have the state governments go after the individuals. Southern states often ignored these requests and, by extension, racial violence. 

It also led to Jim Crow, segregation laws. Since private political parties ran elections, they placed poll taxes (affected all races), literacy tests, grandfather tests (grandfather had voted), and white primaries (only white people could vote in the primary).

Smith v. Allwright made white primaries illegal. Plessy v. Ferguson came about because a black man rode in a whites-only rail car. However, there were blacks-only rail cars. This equality was fine. Thus came the **separate but equal doctrine**. Sweatt v. Painter reaffirmed this stance when two law schools under the same umbrella had to be integrated with one another because there was inequality. Brown v. Board of Education of Topeka overturns Plessy by declaring that separate is inherently unequal.

The Civil Rights Movement of the 1960s culminate in the Civil Rights Act. It sought integration and cracked down on the fake voter restrictions. There was also the Voting Rights Act and Fair Housing Act.

{{< subtext >}}
    The Fair Housing Act also barred discrimination on the basis of sex, religion, national origin, disability, or family status when selling or renting out a home.
{{< /subtext >}}

Heart of Atlanta Motel v. United States came about because the owner refused to rent out to black patrons. The national government argued that, although it is private business, most of the patrons cross state lines, activating the commerce clause. The courts agreed. 

Swann v. Charlotte Mecklenberg School District ruled that de jure segregation must be eliminated, and the lower federal courts have the authority to make it so. De jure segregation is segregation forced by law; de facto segregation happened by coincidence. 



# {{< heading "Women" >}}
Part of the motivation for the initial lack of women's rights (and lack thereof for other groups) is protectionism—that it is for their safety or well-being. 

Minor v. Happersett (1874) came about as women tried to use the equal protection clause of the 14th Amendment to gain suffrage. It was ruled that the amendment only applied to men. This led to the women's suffrage movement and the 19th Amendment, granting them the right to vote. 

The 1964 Civil Rights Act had a small part that made the entire act extend to women. This wasn't treated very seriously, resulting in the women's rights movement. Title IX of Higher Education Act withheld funding from schools and universities that discriminate against women. The Pregnancy discrimination Act and Family and Medical Leave Act prevented unfair firing and demotions for absences due to childbirth or family emergencies. 



# {{< heading "Homosexuality" >}}
Bowers v. Hardwick (1986) ruled that homosexual behavior was not protected by the Constitution. People were not happy, and Lawrence v. Texas (2003) overturns Bowers v. Hardwick with the equal protection clause. Obergefell v. Hodges legalized same sex marriage under equal protection and due process clauses. Bostock v. Clayton County extended the Civil Rights Act to sexual orientation.



# {{< heading "Texas Rights" >}}
Texas does have additional protections beyond the U.S. Constitution. Remonstrance is the right to have some sort of dialogue with government. 

Texas outlaws the use of a religious test for employment (i.e., employing based on religion), though the same can now be said nationwide. There's also more specific rules against discrimination based on religion. Texas does acknowledge a supreme being, but it doesn't enforce that every Texan believes in it.

In regards to the U.S. Constitution Fourth Amendment, Texas requires that descriptions be reasonably specific. For the 14th Amendment, Texas expands on it further by protecting anything that might be used for disenfranchising. 

Texas did ratify the Equal Rights Amendment. The Baby McLean case is actually about a man's equals rights being violated (since he was not married when the child was born, he had no rights over the child). He was ruled with. As a state case, cases dealing with gender fall under strict scrutiny (rather than national's intermediate scrutiny) and suspect classification. This allowed for standardized review to see if ERA had been violated. 

Texas Woman's University v. Chayklintaste is another ERA case that sought equal privileges between sexes.

Texas does have the right to privacy. Texas State Employees Union v. Texas Department of Mental Health and Mental Retardation establishes this right. If there was suspicion, an employee would be forced to take a polygraph. If they refused, they could be disciplined. The Texas courts created a zone of privacy.
