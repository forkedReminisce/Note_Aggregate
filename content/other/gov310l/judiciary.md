Article III only establishes the Supreme Court and gives Congress the ability to establish lower courts. That's it. That's because the Framers didn't fear the threat of tyranny in this branch compared to the other branches. As a result, this branch is the most undemocratic—judges are nominated and serve for life and largely operate without transparency.

{{< subtext >}}
    Judicial review was established in Marbury v. Madison, and it allows the courts determine whether passed laws are constitutional.

    The Framers gave justices life terms so that they wouldn't have to campaign and be at the whims of the public. 
{{< /subtext >}}

Congress checks the judiciary. 
- Alter a court's jurisdiction (the area its ruling apply to)
- Reverse judicial decisions with Constitutional amendments
- Impeach and remove federal judges
- President nominates all federal judges, Congress approves

The national court structure was established by the Judiciary Act of 1789. The lowest level was district courts, followed by the circuits (appellate level), then the Supreme Court. John Marshall and his Marshall Court brought the most significant impact to establishing the judiciary as a co-equal branch. The rulings that come out of this court brought respect and power to the Supreme Court. Judicial review.

{{< subtext >}}
    The Marshall Court was not the first Supreme Court of the U.S.

    Legislative courts specialize in a topic (e.g., taxes). They sit below the Supreme Court. The judges are experts in the particular field with an elected (fixed) term.
{{< /subtext >}}

The legal system is a dual system. There's the state systems and national system. They're separate and parallel (operate concurrently). The state system consists of trial courts followed by the state court of last resort. Half the states have an appellate level. If an appeal of the state court of last resort is disliked, the appeal can go to the Supreme Court. The national system consists of constitutional courts that are concerned about matters of US code or the Constitution. 94 district courts, can appeal to one of 13 Courts of Appeals. Circuits are assigned a Supreme Court justice, who will decide whether this appealed case is worth hearing. The Rule of Four is that three other justices agree to hear, then a writ of certiorari issued to request all the documents of the case. They then decide then whether to actually hear the case. In forma pauperis is where the Supreme Court generously chooses to cover the filing fees and such for the case.

{{< subtext >}}
    The Solicitor General's Office argues on behalf of the United States in front of the Supreme Court.

    There are nine Supreme Court justices.
{{< /subtext >}}

Criminal cases is over breaking the law. Civil cases are between individuals and can be compensatory (paying back) or punitive (paying back the side effects too). Class action suit is when a class sues a party, and the ruling applies to the class. Standing (per situation) is the right to sue. Amicus curiae allows an outside party of a case to expose their opinion to the judges and juries. A test case is a medium a group of people use to determine if now is a good time to be exploring certain ideas (basically a burner case). The opinion of the court or plurality opinion is the majority opinion and it is the binding opinion. A justice may add a concurring opinion to the opinion of the court to explain their different reason for voting the same way. Similarly, the dissenting opinion for voting the opposite way. A per curiam opinion is anonymous in a way; delivered by the court as a whole.  

{{< subtext >}}
    The court might offer the criminal a plea bargain. They plead guilty and get reduced charges. This is because there's so many cases and things need to be kept moving.

    A class is a group of people in a similar situation.
{{< /subtext >}} 

Judicial activism is judges shaping the law of the land based on personal interpretations of the Constitution (e.g., judicial review). Judicial restraint is not doing anything because the law is constitutional. Original intention describes judges that interpret the Constitution based on the Framers' intention. # {{< heading "Supreme Court Powers" >}}
Original jurisdiction courts hear evidence, take testimony, deliver rulings; the court is the first one to be hearing the case. Appellate jurisdiction cover a case the court hears *on appeal*. Refusing to hear a case makes it so that whatever court last heard it will give the final ruling.

{{< subtext >}}
    The trial court is where the original jurisdiction take place. Single judge. The appellate courts examine trial court materials. Multi-judge panel.

    Appeals can only be made for judicial rights violations (e.g., Fifth Amendment, right to avoid self-incrimination). If the rights were violated, the ruling will be vacated and have the trial court correct the ruling. Since this process ensures that the case is handled fine, new evidence that would've changed the ruling do not matter. This is why pardons exist.
{{< /subtext >}}

Statutory Construction describes how the Supreme Court helps create a law by interpreting or reinterpreting a state or federal law. As values and thinking develop over time, we have a living constitution as it can be interpreted differently now than it did back then. If the Supreme Court strikes down the law, it is engaging in either constitutional interpretation or statutory interpretation depending on whether the issue is the constitution or laws.
