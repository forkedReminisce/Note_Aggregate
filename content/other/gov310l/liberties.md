Civil Liberties are freedoms protected from governmental interference. Think the First Amendment. The Ninth Amendment is critical because it admits that the Constitution does not exhaustively cover all the civil liberties, but they're still protected. 



# {{< heading "Judiciary" >}}
Barron V. Baltimore (1833) was a case about property rights and how they were infringed on by the state. The *imminent domain* clause of the Fifth Amendment was tried, but it was rejected because it didn't extend to the states. This changed with the 14th Amendment a few decades later. 

Gitlow v. New York was a shift that saw civil liberty cases expand beyond economics and into social aspects. Part of the ruling stated that states could not abridge free speech. Making the states abide by the Bill of Rights is known as *Selective Incorporation*. Despite the ratification of the 14th Amendment, the states could still disregard civil liberties. In order for a particular civil liberty to be protected from the states, it had to be sued over.

{{< subtext >}}
    Not every amendment of the Bill of Rights has been incorporated. This is because the opportunity to sue has never arose (e.g., Third Amendment). 
{{< /subtext >}}

Cases will have a different set of assumptions based on a few heuristics. Most cases fall under *Ordinary Scrutiny*, which assumes that the government is abiding by the law and it is the plaintiff's job to prove that what they're doing is illegal. On the flip side, *Strict Scrutiny* assumes the government is violating the Constitution, and they must prove that they are not (e.g., things about race). *Intermediate Scrutiny* falls in the middle because the heuristics are not enough.



# {{< heading "Freedom Of Speech" >}}
The Italian Hall Disaster (1913) resulted in a sizable number of deaths, many children, because someone untruthfully yelled, "Fire!" Schenck v. United States (1919) made free speech that can cause a clear and present danger unprotected.

Fighting Words (1942) was a Supreme Court case that made unprotected speech that is provocative and can cause a fight. Brandenburg v. Ohio (1969) provides no protection for direct incitement, which is encouraging illegal action. Snyder v. Phelps (2011) affirmed that offensive speech is protected as long as the execution was legal. 

Governments can regulate peaceable assembly if they don't play any favorites. 

Miller v. California (1973) establishes the Miller test to determine if the content is classified as obscenity. First, the average person must find that it appeals to lust. Next, it fits the state definition of sexual conduct. Third, it must lack value (e.g., artistic). 

Libel is written false statements. Slander is spoken. Either can damage a person's reputation. Prior Restrain allows the government to prohibit media from publishing certain materials. Commercial speech can be regulated if it is illegal, misleading, or there is a good reason for the government to step in otherwise (doesn't include offensive speech).

{{< subtext >}}
    Public figures fall under parody, and to qualify for libel or slander, malice has to be proven.
{{< /subtext >}}

Freedom of religion is provided by two clauses. The *Free Exercise Clause* prohibits the government from interfering with the practice of religion. This includes freedom to believe and mostly freedom of action (e.g., no sacrifices). The *Establishment Clause* prohibits any hint of an official religion from as high as Congress to the teacher in the classroom. Lemon v. Kurtzman (1971) established the Lemon Test to ensure not establishing religion: the law must have a secular (non-religious) purpose, must neither advance or diminish religion, and must not excessively entangle state and religion. However, the courts no longer use this test and instead just determine in the moment. 



# {{< heading "Right To Bear Arms" >}}
The Second Amendment was originally written without the people in mind, but rather state militias. That's why there was very little regulation on guns until the National Firearms Act (1935). Since this required firearms to be registered and the associated tax was obscenely large, U.S. v. Miller came. The ruling was that ==the Second Amendment only applied to military-type weapons==. 

The Second Amendment was incorporated in District of Columbia v. Heller (2008) and McDonald v. Chicago (2010). Both of these cases arose from local laws attempting to put restrictions on gun ownership. It was upheld that restrictions are not necessarily unconstitutional, though.



# {{< heading "Other Amendments" >}}
The Fourth Amendment is the right against unreasonable searches. It is incorporated in Mapp v. Ohio (1961). It established the exclusionary rule, which prohibits police and prosecutors from using evidence obtained in an illegal search. Not every unwarranted search is illegal as long as there is probable cause. For example, eventual discovery is when evidence is discovered unintentionally. The Open Fields Doctrine allows observations of one's private open field to be valid evidence.

The Fifth Amendment includes the right against self-incrimination. Miranda v. Arizona (1966) introduced the idea of the Miranda rights and that suspects must be aware of them. The Miranda rights are the right to remain silent, anything said can and will be used against, right to speak to an attorney, and if one cannot be afforded, one will be provided. Double jeopardy, trying someone twice for the same crime, is not allowed, even if evidence proving guilt is discovered.

The Sixth Amendment grants rights to the accused in criminal prosecutions.
- Speedy trial
- Jury comprised of those in the area of the crime
- Be made aware of what the trial is about
- To know who is the accuser, unless a certain circumstance is met
- Gather witness of their own
- Assistance of Counsel: to consult, not have, a lawyer

The Eighth Amendment is cruel and unusual punishment. The death penalty was in a gray area for a while. Inherently, it is not cruel and unusual as long as the method of execution wasn't originally designed for torture. Furman v. Georgia (1972) struck down Georgia's death penalty because who received the death penalty was unfair. When Georgia rewrote it, Gregg v. Georgia (1976) upheld their new death penalty. Ring v. Arizona (2002) ruled that only juries can decide on the death penalty. 

Privacy is not mentioned in the Constitution, but it is a penumbral (i.e., judicially created right). A lot of it comes from cases about reproductive rights. Griswold v. Connecticut (1965) established the idea of marital privacy, meaning that married couples have a right to private communication. Roe v. Wade divided the right to abortion across three stages of pregnancy. In the first stage, the mother's right to privacy gave her the absolute right. The states could intervene by the second stage, only to protect her health. For the third stage, if the fetus is viable, it has person rights. It can still be terminated, though, if the mother's life is in danger. Webster v. Reproductive Health Services ruled that if the fetus was viable by the second stage, it has person rights. This approach was abandoned in Planned Parenthood of Southeastern Pennsylvania v. Casey, and instead judged abortion regulations on whether or not they impose an undue burden upon pregnant women. Dobbs v. Jackson Women's Health Organization overruled these previous cases by making abortion a state issue rather than a national one.

    The Hyde Amendment prohibits the use of Medicaid for abortions.
