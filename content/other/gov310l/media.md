# {{< heading "Public Opinion" >}}
To gauge public opinion on politics and government actions, **scientific polling** is employed. A population is the group being studied, and a sample is a subset of the population that will be used to represent the group. To select a sample, random sampling is most ideal. That way, extrapolated results are most accurate.

Aggregate Public Opinion is the sum of all individual opinions. Extremes tend to get muted if there's enough randomness in the sampling. Political ideology is consistent. 

There is sampling error and sampling bias. Sampling bias is when the sample doesn't best represent the population. For example, oversampling a subgroup or polling at a time where answers are likely uncertain. Measurement error is when the question is misinterpreted (misheard). 

{{< subtext >}}
    Statistical dead heat is when the sampling error introduces a toss-up in the results and makes it impossible to make a conclusion.
{{< /subtext >}}

Push-polling is when a particular conclusion is reached by painting a particular opinion in a bad light. This exposes the idea of *framing* and how it can artificially produce certain conclusions. Wording, question order (the first question sets mood), and whether it's open-ended or closed-ended (misses nuances). 

Political socialization is how a person's opinions are shaped by other people and their surroundings. If we don't really care about a topic, we tend to give latent opinions. Alternatively, we follow the opinions of our opinion leaders—people we think know their stuff. However, this does motivate politicians to overly simplify issues (e.g., "abortion is bad"). Another issue of *cognitive shortcuts* is that partisanship introduces confirmation bias towards media.



# {{< heading "Mass Media" >}}
Mass media are organizations that widely distribute information, as in they don't up to person to person. How they frame politicians and issues can influence their viewers' opinions. The Communications Act of 1934 had the *Equal Time Provision*, which if a political candidate buys air time, their competitors must be offered the same time for the same price. 

{{< subtext >}}
    There are laws against disinformation.

    The Equal Time Provision is suspended during debates.
{{< /subtext >}}

In 1949, the *Fairness Doctrine* required that that media had to give air time to both sides of an issue. However, the prospect of presenting the opposite of an unanimous opinion led media to not touch certain topics. This led to the end of the doctrine in 1987. What happened next was the rise of programs that leaned heavily conservative or liberal.

Media, especially trusted stations, influence a citizen's opinions on a topic. Of course, there is framing. Then there is filtering, what the media chooses to report, and priming, where the media pedals an opinion so much that the viewer begins to share the same opinion. Slant is this opinion.

Most of our media is consolidated by a few big companies. This begs to question what the media might be hiding. Local stations are also more likely to have the big company do their work, eliminating local messages. A benefit, however, is that smaller stations can survive under the umbrella when they otherwise would've died.
