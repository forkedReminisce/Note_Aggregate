Politics is the use of authority to allocate scare resources and settle conflicts. *Authority* is the capacity to direct the behavior of others. This comes in the form of **legitimate coercion**. 

Coercion is necessary to uphold the political order. Domestically, a monopoly on coercion is crucial to dispelling rebels. There are even greater implications on the international level. With the ability to threaten war, states can discourage other states from messing with them. Additionally, military power helps determine every state's place in the international system.

However, granting political organizations the capacity for coercion comes with the risk of it being used for predation. This is known as the *coercion dilemma*. Democracy solves this issue on the domestic front, while the international system requires retaliatory military force. If this military force needs to be maintained after the conflict, it can be legitimated by members recognizing a continued threat, approval through democracy, and consulting multilateral alliances.



# {{< heading "War" >}}
War is costly. Borders change and there are refugee flows, which end up altering the demographic composition of the state. More horrifically, people die and the economy gets destroyed.

The external security threats of war can create an opportunity for the government to expand. Raising taxes, expanding the bureaucracy, and heightening restrictions on liberties; citizens willingly gives these up for security. However, there's always the flip side. The longer the war goes on, the more frustrated citizens will be of the costs. This leads to a rise in opposition, which can sometimes result in revolution.

## {{< heading "Bargaining Model Of War" >}}
The **Bargaining Model of War** is the framework that explains why war starts. It rests on the assumption that ==war is costly==: human costs, destruction of the infrastructure of the economy, and opportunity costs of the economy. If peace leaves all parties better off, then one must think about what is preventing a peace settlement.

*Private information* is one reason. It is a party's unknowingness of the other party's military capabilities or resolve—willingness to pay the costs of war. This causes the party to overestimate its bargaining leverage. Another reason is the *commitment problem*, where parties are skeptical of the other's commitment to a deal in the long term and may, therefore, not agree to the terms.

All wars have a conflict of interest. Most of the time, it's over a resource. ==However, there is always a range of settlements that leaves both parties better off==. War breaks out because there's a bargaining failure, which are the two reasons described above. 

<!-- concessions less than the expected utility of war means going to war -->
When war offers a better deal than bargaining, parties turn to war. Through war, they can impose costs on the other party that will pressure the other party to make concessions. Parties measure the value of war with the *expected utility of war*: weighing likelihood of winning (based on military capabilities) minus the potential costs of going to war. 

Private information can create a mismatch of expected utility between the two parties. The party with the secret advantage may demand greater concessions, which could provoke the other party as their expected utility hasn't been properly adjusted. And if the other party sticks with its bargaining mistake, it could provoke this party. Revealing private information opens the door to lie, which can mistakably decrease the likelihood of wining for the other side. However, bluffs get called often. The alternative that works is costly signals—a party pays some costs to convey private information. Audience costs are being public and setting the standard to get punished if it is not followed through, while sunk costs are a subtle demonstration of power. Liars typically won't choose to pay these costs. 

Commitment problem is when a party thinks any peace deal will result in the other party demanding more concessions in the future. During this supposed time of peace, the distribution of power will shift towards the other side. Therefore, the declining side finds it logical to fight now before it gets relatively weaker—preventative war. 

Institutional mechanisms can make further demands by the stronger side less attractive. Arms control agreements stabilizes distribution of power. Democracy also stabilizes distribution of power as the people elect and control military spending. Membership in international organizations can signify to the other side some set of expectations (e.g., being in NATO implies no plan to go to war).
