The U.S. operates as a democracy. This means the people have a voice in government to some capacity. A direct democracy is when they vote on matters themselves, while an indirect or representative democracy has representatives. More precisely, the U.S. is a democratic republic. A republic just means that there is some restriction on who can participate (i.e., over the age of 18).

{{< subtext >}}
    The U.S. is also a constitutional democracy, meaning the national government gets its powers from the Constitution.
{{< /subtext >}}

**Federalism** is another system the U.S. is under. Power is equally divided between the federal government and smaller entities (i.e., states). The plus-sides to this are:
- Minimizes policy conflict: pass a law at the state level first
- Closer to the people
- State laws with the state's population in mind
- Local governments can be a politician's training ground

{{< subtext >}}
    Interstate compacts are agreements between states that would otherwise be a national issue.
{{< /subtext >}}

That is not to say that federalism is without concerns, though. Quality of life is inconsistent between states, local governments don't get media attention, and potential cutthroat competition and race to the bottom.



# {{< heading "Types" >}}
Dual federalism describes a hierarchy between the different level of governments. Each level may have multiple governments (e.g., Texas and California as state governments), and they are separate and distinct from one another. Issues below don't concern the above. 

{{< subtext >}}
    The U.S. was under this system until the 30s.
{{< /subtext >}}

Cooperative federalism finds the higher levels helping the lower levels with their issues. They do this through planning and funding. ==Our national government follows this== because local issues could become national ones and to minimize gaps between states. There are two rules to ensuring these goals: prevent spillover effects (issues of one state spreading to a neighboring state) and give money to state and local agencies (they can act appropriately for their population).

{{< subtext >}}
    Picket Fence federalism is just cooperative federalism but emphasizes organization.

    Competitive federalism describes governments of the same level competing to attract businesses.
{{< /subtext >}}

Money is given out via grants. Block grants concern a topic, but are vague. Categorical grants are more specific. Program grants concern an immediate issue. Formula grants are decided by a formula.

{{< subtext >}}
    An unfunded mandate is where the federal government threatens penalities if a state doesn't do a certain thing ==unfunded==.

    Coercive federalism finds that money is only given out if the smaller bodies do a task. Categorical grants often have objectives attached to them.
{{< /subtext >}}


Other than federalism, there is unitary and confederal. The former gives more power to the federal government, while the latter to the smaller structures.



# {{< heading "Dividing Power" >}}
Former Supreme Court Chief Justice John Marshall established judicial review. This separated the Supreme Court from lower level courts.

Leading up the the Civil War, southern states tried to use nullification, a supposed state power to declare laws, including federal, unconstitutional, to keep slaves. This did not work because of the Supremacy Clause.

The Reservation Clause (10th Amendment) gave the powers not explicitly granted nor prohibited to the national government to the states and people. 

The 14th Amendment was a Civil War Amendment, and it offered:
- Double citizenship: state and national citizenship is linked
- Due Process Clause: states must also protect life, liberty, and property
- Remedial legislation: overwrite discriminatory state laws
- Privileges and Immunities Clause: state cannot make laws stripping away privileges and immunities

There are even more things that set the national government apart from state governments. Preemption allows it to overrule the laws of a state (that don't conflict with national laws) with its own, all judges must follow the Constitution, and the national government must protect the states from invasion and violence. 

Concurrent powers are possessed by the national government and state governments. There are also denied powers—not held by either entity. Some denied powers are:
- Bill of Attainer: prohibits sending someone to jail without trial 
- Ex Post Facto laws: prevents punishing someone of the current penalty for having done something in the past that was previously legal but now illegal
