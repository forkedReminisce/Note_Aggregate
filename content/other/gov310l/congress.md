Congress is the **legislative branch** of the three branches of U.S. government. It's bicameral, meaning two chambers: the House of Representatives and Senate. 



# {{< heading "Legislators" >}}
Legislators can fall into three categories. A delegate works for the interests of their constituents regardless of what they personally believe. A trustee votes for what they think will be best for their constituents. What most legislators in the U.S. are are politicos, which are delegates for important issues and trustees for everything else.

{{< subtext >}}
    Constituents do not only refer to the voters, but everyone in the region.
{{< /subtext >}}

Legislators are obligated to carry out casework—help out with constituent problems. Word of mouth is crucial for their reelection, which they already have a lot of advantage as an incumbent. The franking privilege is when they promote their accomplishments while in office. They'll also find it easier to raise money as there is a lot of confidence behind their victory. Pork barrel and earmarks are taking credit for public projects in the local region obtained by stuffing a bill.

{{< subtext >}}
    An open seat is a position without an incumbent running.

    Donors believe they can get favors when the incumbent wins reelection.
{{< /subtext >}}



# {{< heading "House Of Representatives" >}}
The House of Representatives is a terminal body. Meaning every two years, *all 435 chairs* are put up for election.

Each House member represents a state district. Every district in a state must have the same number of people, not only voters. However, state legislatures tend to skew the representation in various ways:
- Gerrymandering: drawing the district lines in a way to get safe seats for the party
- Cracking: minimizes impact of the other party by breaking apart their region and putting it with friendlier regions
- Packing: coalescing regions of the other party into a few districts

{{< subtext >}}
    These practices are illegal.
{{< /subtext >}}

The House is meant to be the closest to the people. They are the first point of contact to the national government and any bills modifying taxes must start here (this power is intended to be close with the people).

The House Democratic Caucus and House Republican Conference are former terms to describe the party a House member is in. Party decisions (e.g., committee assignments and strategies) happen here. 

The Speaker of the House is a constitutional position—enshrined in the Constitution. They are selected via internal election, so they tend to be of the majority party. Their job is to lead the House; they assign bills to committee and calendar.

{{< subtext >}}
    The Speaker can indirectly kill a bill by assigning it to an inappropriate committee.
{{< /subtext >}}

Each party has a party leader and a whip. The whip, in particular, provides bill summaries, advise their party members on how to vote, and do nose counts—head count and vote count.

{{< subtext >}}
    Whips also can know where a party member is at any time. Since they do nose counts, they can tell if the party will need more votes, and they can get more party members to come in.
{{< /subtext >}}

Unique to the House is the Rules Committee. They decide the maximum time a bill can be debated. They also put restrictions on who can make amendments to the bill; closed rule is only to members of the original committee, restricted rule is more relaxed, and open rule is open to everyone. 

{{< subtext >}}
    Nongermane amendments (or riders) are not related to the bill. They are not allowed.
{{< /subtext >}}



# {{< heading "Senate" >}}
The Senate is a continual body, meaning only 1/3 of the 100 seats go up for election every two years. The intention is to have institutional memory that serves the foundation of the changing House and presidency. 

Equivalent to the Speaker of the House is the majority leader. They, and the minority leader, also persuade their party members to vote a particular way. They also have whips.

The president pro tempore is the most senior senator of the majority party. They coordinate with staff and guide the new senators. Then there's the vice president, who just puts in the tiebreaker vote on bills.

Some conventions in the Senate are unanimous consent (used to pass boring bills) and holds (a senator can delay a vote on a bill to "better educate themselves" when in reality they don't want to face the backlash of their constituents).



# {{< heading "Turning A Bill Into Law" >}}
When a bill gets introduced to the clerks, it gets assigned a number (HR for the House and S for Senate).

On the House side, the Speaker will refer the bill to a (sub-)committee. The committee will hold hearings to understand the bill. The (sub-)committee will then do mark up on the bill. They will then hold a vote to report the bill out. The speaker will then calendar the bill. On the scheduled day, a floor reading and debate (including amendments, if permitted) will take place. The full vote will follow to produce the engrossed bill.

{{< subtext >}}
    A bill is tabled (and effectively killed) if the committee hearings were bad.

    A bill that doesn't get reported out can get to full vote through the discharge petition (the majority of the House must sign it). The Senate does not have this.
{{< /subtext >}}

The differences on the Senate side are on the day of full vote. Firstly, it is always open rule. Secondly, the filibuster: senators can continuously debate. A stop to this can be made through a cloture vote, which makes the final vote inevitable. Since getting 60 senators to agree is difficult, filibustering is a huge threat since it blocks the other bills in queue. So non-filibustering senators may make amendments to make progress. 

{{< subtext >}}
    Any bills that can't become law fast enough before the House session ends will die.
{{< /subtext >}}

The conference committee is comprised of members of both chambers. Their job is to merge the engrossed bills for the enrolled bill (numbered Bill). This enrolled bill goes back to both chambers for one more full vote. Then it goes to the president. If the president vetos the bill, it goes back to both chambers for a veto override, which requires a 2/3s vote. 

{{< subtext >}}
    If the president ignores the bill, it becomes law in 30 days. However, if Congress passed the enrolled bill in the last ten days of session, the same action results in a pocket veto and kills the bill.
{{< /subtext >}}

On committees, there are three other types. The one working on bills in their chamber are select committees. These are permanent and chairmanship is held by a member of the majority party. The ranking member would be chair if their party was the majority party. 

Select committees investigate issues or wrongdoing, while joint committees are comprised of members of both chambers to handle Congress-as-a-whole responsibilities. 

There are also peculiar practices in this law-making procedure. Log rolling sees two members agree to vote for each other's amendments. Omnibus legislation combines many small bills into a big bill, speeding things up. However, things can sneak in. 

Finally, budget bills has a start and end date. If a new budget bill isn't passed by the end date, a government shutdown ensues. This just means non-essential workers don't go to work. A continuing resolution solves this by allowing the government to use the old budget for an amount of time.



# {{< heading "Impeachment" >}}
Treason, bribery, and abuse of power (but can be not criminal) are impeachable. 

The House Judiciary Committee can perform an investigation first, or the House can just jump straight to the Full Floor vote. In any case, the vote requires a simple majority. The impeachment cannot be expunged (although, it's never been tested).

Then the Senate holds the impeachment trial. The Chief Justice is in charge of the trial, a team from the House, called managers, presents evidence, a defense counsel (e.g., lawyers) will represent the person, and senators make up the jury. If 2/3s of the Senate vote yes, the person is immediately removed from office without appeal.
