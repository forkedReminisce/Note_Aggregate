Particles are a crucial part of Japanese; they mark important details to the sentence or **just serve adverbial or conjunctive functions**. The latter is the focus of this article.



# {{< heading "\"And\"" >}}
**と joins two *nouns* with "and."** This may be chained, but every subsequent と is replaced with a comma. Alternatively, every と is replaced with ・. Regardless of how it's written, /to/ will be pronounced every time.

"I am Japanese and a doctor" is actually short for "I am Japanese and I am a doctor." Therefore, と would not be applicable despite looking so. There are other ways to cover the other functions of the English "and," one of which being **そして**. It's used to immediately build on top of information. Think "and" at the start of a sentence or "then." 

と creates **complete lists**, suggesting that there are no *omitted* items that are satisfiable. **や**'s purpose is to create incomplete lists. When the items are mutually exclusive, や sometimes translates to "or." The effect of using や is that each entity's independence is lowered as compared to と. や also pairs really well with predicates with a wide scope or when the speaker wants to direct the listener's attention to the information following the list (even when the list is complete). It is also the case that because と often introduces its entities for the first time as new information, や goes with old information.

{{% example_jp
    jp="「山椒魚は、杉苔や銭苔を眺めることを好まなかった。」"
    en="\"*The salamander did not care for gazing at the cedar moss or liverwort*.\""
%}}

など often follows the final noun in an incomplete list to reference omitted-yet-still-applicable items. のような appears sometimes instead. など does not require an explicit list, though; it can attach to a noun or verb to reference a list that includes said noun or verb. なんか is a colloquial variant in this regard. There are also the variants なんど, なぞ, and なんぞ. Other particles will appear before these3 particles, except が and を do not appear at all.

**し** is like や but for full blown clauses. It attaches to the parts of speech without modification; it may even attach to ～ます, but only really in spoken speech. The clauses (and alike ideas) are related such that they contribute to an overarching idea. 

{{< subtext >}}
    し cannot suggest a chronological order; that is reserved for the て-form.
{{< /subtext >}}



# {{< heading "\"Also\"" >}}
<!-- chaining (e.g., XもYも linked to the same thing) as a term sucks. replace it.  -->
**も** marks nouns, adjectives, and adjectival nouns that **too share a quality** to something. Adjectives conjugate as ～くも and adjectival nouns as ～でも. Multiple も make that "something" each other. If there are multiple *disjoint* も in the same sentence, the linked thing can be different between them. If the noun is the topic, subject, or direct object, も absorbs those particles. But it doesn't absorb every particle.
<!-- 美人でもなく（て）かわいくもない人に一目惚れすることはあるのか。 when used with も, it seems that て can be omitted -->

{{< subtext >}}
    も softens the overall tone of the sentence.

    も can translate to "even." This is especially true with negative predicates.
{{< /subtext >}}

{{% example_jp
    jp="「**僕も**お腹が空いた。」"
    en="\"***I**'m hungry, **too***.\""
%}}

When after counter expressions, も interprets reaching (and potentially exceeding) the amount as absurd in the speaker's mind. は is similar but it isn't all dramatic about it. This gets flipped on its head with negative sentences, with the quantity now being a threshold that isn't surpassed. Of course, も implies the current situation is way short, whereas は is just stative. The dramatics can modify a noun using もの.

By attaching も to the て-form, "even" is created. It also often lessens the tone of the overall sentence. Putting いい, かまわない, or さしっかえない after demonstrates permission, and も may be removed to be more direct. 何といっても means "no matter what they say." Verbal predicates can attach でも to their て-form to say that the main clause (which shows intention) should or will be carried regardless of what this clause says. 

{{< subtext >}}
    Adjectival nouns use ても.
    
    Nouns can use でも like it were a particle.

    ～ないでも is uncommon.
{{< /subtext >}}



# {{< heading "Reason" >}}
**し** indicates causes for the main clause. It is placed like it's "and" function, but there may also just be one cause. Additionally, some causes may simply be omitted. When し follows のだ or んだ, the clause serves as the main cause. The main clause does not necessarily have to follow the causes, in which case, し does not follow the final cause. However, in colloquial speech, it may be a final particle because the main clause is omitted and left for the listener to infer or a tone softener so that the listener doesn't think about the causes too much. The clauses may alternatively be effects and the main clause is the cause. In this case, だから may precede the first result or から or ので gets put onto the (preceding) main clause.

{{% example_jp
    jp="「**ちょっと用事がありますし**、お先に帰らせていただきます。」"
    en="\"***I got some chores**, so I’ll be heading home first*.\""
%}}



# {{< heading "\"Only\"" >}}
**だけ** attaches to clauses to say that it is the **only option**. Nouns, adjectives, adjectival nouns, and verbs may be the predicate that gets attached to, but adjectival nouns require な go in between. It may also appear at the sentence end with the inclusion of a copula predicate. だけでなく means "not only." なるべく and できるだけ may be used adverbially to mean "if possible" or "as much as possible." The former is more formal, and the latter may modify nouns with の. 

{{< subtext >}}
    If the clause is the subject or direct object, the respective particles may be omitted.

    If at sentence end, のこと before the copula makes the sentence more forceful. Without the copula, the sentence is less blunt.
    
    だけではなく is possible.

    なるべく can use の as なるたけ or なるだけ, but it's rare.
{{< /subtext >}}

{{% example_jp
    jp="「テストのことを思うだけで不安になった。」"
    en="\"*I became uneasy just at the thought of the test*.\""
%}}
