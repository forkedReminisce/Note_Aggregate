Single-view ambiguity describes how a point on a 2D image could have been projected from an infinite number of 3D points on the ray. A good way of finding the exact 3D point is stereo: triangulating with two cameras and finding where the rays intersect. Alternatively, move. Pictorial cues like shading, perspective effects, and familiar objects are another strategy. These make up some of the common geometry problems:
- Calibration: figure out intrinsic properties of the camera \(K\) that can be used to derive rays
- Recovering structure
- Stereo or Epipolar geometry: given two cameras, find the original point
- Motion: figure out the correspondences between cameras

The projected 2D point is the camera intrinsics multiplied by the rotation-translation matrix multiplied with the 3D point. Camera intrinsics is 

\[
    \begin{bmatrix}
    f & 0 & u_0 \\
    0 & f & v_0 \\
    0 & 0 & 1
    \end{bmatix}
\]

The rotation-translation matrix is the concatenated \(3 \times 3\) rotation matrix and \(3 \times 1\) translation matrix. The first multiplication can be described as \(M_{3 \times 4}\). The 3D point is \(4 \times 1\) because it's in homogenous coordinates. 

By following from homographies footsteps, it's possible to solve for \(M\) using least squares. From there, it is possible to use QR decomposition to get \(K\), \(R\) and \(t\). Least squares is overconstrained, so it is preferred to use the result to initialize an ML model. 

Solving for \(X\) instead has geometric or optimization solution methods. These are quite tedious. 

Vanishing points are when parallel lines in the 3D space are no longer parallel in the 2D space. They eventually converge at a point called the vanishing point. A line between the vanishing points on the same plane exist called the vanishing line.

A projective invariant is a quantity that does not change under projective transformations. The cross-ratio of four points on the same line distances to one another is the same whether the points are 3D or 2D projection. This allows one to compute distances between 3D points (e.g., height).

{{< subtext >}}
    The fourth point may be at infinity in the 3D space. Its projected point may be off the image.
